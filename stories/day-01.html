Day 1 · Mon 28 Sep · Melbourne → Copenhagen

Stories, myths & legends

Copenhagen has been telling stories about itself for a thousand years. Some are true. Some are Andersen. Some are what a sailor told his wife when he came home late. All of them are still repeated, which is what matters.

Why this sectionBeyond history — the things a taxi-driver would tell you if you asked why a street is called what it's called. Every entry is either folk-legend, well-attested rumour, or a story locals still believe on Wednesdays.

The Little Mermaid's real ending

Andersen · 1837

Disney gave her a wedding. Andersen gave her a knife. In the original tale, the sea-witch offers the mermaid one way home — kill the prince at dawn and let his blood run over her feet, and she becomes a mermaid again. She stands over him sleeping, hesitates, throws the knife into the sea, and dissolves into sea foam. Then — this is the part everyone forgets — she rises as a "daughter of the air", and if she does 300 years of quiet good deeds, she earns a human soul. Andersen wrote it the year he was ghosted by Edvard Collin, the man he'd written most of his love letters to.

Nyhavn 20 and the tinderbox

1834 · Andersen's first Copenhagen address

Andersen moved into Nyhavn 20 in 1834, poor and unpublished, and wrote The Tinderbox in a single week — a story about a soldier who finds a magic tinderbox that summons three dogs with eyes the size of teacups, mill-wheels, and the Round Tower. Locals will tell you (with a wink) that a real tinderbox was fished out of the canal outside No. 20 the year the story was published. There isn't one in any museum, but the story survives, which in Copenhagen is the same thing.

Christian IV walks when Denmark is in danger

Rosenborg · died 1648

The old king built Rosenborg himself and died there. Palace staff have been reporting for 400 years that his footsteps are heard in the Long Hall the night before Denmark faces something serious. He was seen (they say) the week before the 1864 war with Prussia, again in August 1914, and once more on April 8, 1940 — the night before the German invasion. The state does not comment on ghosts. The staff do.

The nameless queen in the pearls

Rosenborg Treasury

Among the crown jewels is a rope of black South Sea pearls said to have belonged to Queen Sophie Amalie (d. 1685), an imperious woman disliked by the court. Three queens who tried them on afterwards died within a year, and by 1720 the pearls were kept in a locked drawer marked only "Do not wear." They are on display today, but no royal woman has worn them since. Curators, when pressed, will say only that "the rope is fragile."

The Ghost Line under Kastrup

M2 metro · driverless since 2007

The M2 has no driver, so its front window looks straight into the tunnel — which is why drivers of passing trains on the parallel track have, since 2008, occasionally reported a figure standing at the front of the northbound carriage. Metro operations explains, correctly, that any lit carriage becomes a mirror at speed and any silhouette is your own reflection. This explanation has never stopped the drivers from calling it the Ghost Line.

Told to the little one · The Sandman

Ole Lukøje · Andersen 1841

Ole Lukøje ("Ole Shut-eye") tiptoes into children's rooms at night with two umbrellas. Over good children he opens the pretty one, painted with stories, and they dream in colour. Over the naughty ones he opens the plain black one, and they sleep with no dreams at all. Danish parents still tell it. Ask her which umbrella she'd like tonight.
