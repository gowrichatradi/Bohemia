Day 1 · Mon 28 Sep · Melbourne → Copenhagen

Stories, myths & legends

Copenhagen doesn't shout. It leans in and tells you something that shouldn't be true, and then hands you the proof. A tsar on a horse inside a tower. A clock whose slowest gear will finish its first full turn in the year 27,777. Two Nobel medals hidden by a chemist in a small jar of yellow acid. A whole neighbourhood of tiny yellow houses that has been home to the same profession for four hundred years.

Why this sectionBeyond the history — the kind of stories a Copenhagener tells you at the second beer. Half are true, half are legend, and the city has stopped caring which is which. That is part of the fun.

The little yellow houses that have been home for 400 years

Nyboder · Christian IV · built 1631

Just north of the old fortifications is a whole neighbourhood of tiny yellow terraced houses lined up in immaculate rows, each with a red front door and a lamp above it. This is Nyboder. It was built by Christian IV in 1631 as housing for the ordinary sailors of the Danish Navy and their families — one of the earliest examples of purpose-built public housing anywhere in the world. It is still working. Almost four hundred years later, the same houses are still occupied by the same profession: you can only rent one if you or your spouse is in some way connected to the Royal Danish Navy. Same rules. Same paint. Same brass number plates on the same doors. Walk down Sankt Paulsgade. Every house is minutely different from its neighbour, hand-built over nineteen years by hundreds of shipwrights, and if you look up at the roofline you can just about see where they gave up matching their heights and let the terrace do what it liked. It has been doing what it liked ever since.

The clock that will finish in the year 27,777

Jens Olsen's World Clock · Copenhagen City Hall

In a small room in Copenhagen City Hall stands one of the most extraordinary mechanical objects ever built. Jens Olsen was a locksmith who taught himself astronomy from library books. He spent forty years designing a clock that could show, at once, local time, sidereal time, the time in sixty-two cities around the world, the phase of the moon, the tides, the sunrise and sunset for every latitude, the day of the Gregorian calendar, the Chinese lunar year, and the drift of the pole star across the sky. It has fifteen thousand parts. Every gear is hand-cut. Every hand is hand-forged. It has fourteen movements running independently, and it has been ticking since 1955 without ever being reset. Jens did not live to see it finished — he died in 1945 with the design complete and the metal not yet cut — and his students carried on and started it up on his birthday. The slowest gear in the mechanism turns once every 25,753 years. It will complete its first full revolution some time around the year 27,777, and there is no reason to think the clock will have stopped by then. Stand in front of it. It is quietly the longest project anyone has ever finished.

The tsar who rode a horse up a tower

Rundetårn · 1716

The Round Tower has no stairs inside. It has a ramp — wide, gently spiralling, seven and a half turns from the door to the observatory on the roof. It was built that way in 1642 so that horses could haul astronomical instruments to the top. In 1716 Tsar Peter the Great, two metres tall and a show-off by any standard, decided the ramp could also carry a rider. He rode his horse straight up. His wife Catherine, refusing to be second in anything, followed in a carriage drawn by six horses that scraped its wheel hubs on the walls. There is a plaque about it inside the door, and the scrape marks are still there, because no one has ever repainted over history in Copenhagen.

The king who wore his own cannon

Christian IV · Battle of Kolberger Heide · 1644

During a naval battle against the Swedes, a cannon on Christian IV's own ship burst next to him and a splinter of metal took out his right eye. The king wiped his face, gave the order to keep firing, and won the day. What he did afterwards is the part nobody has ever quite believed. He collected the tiny splinters of metal that had hit him, sent them to his royal jeweller, and had them set into a pair of gold earrings that he wore for the rest of his life. Every portrait of him in old age shows him wearing them. It was, in effect, a small piece of the battle turned into jewellery, worn as a reminder that the ship had held. Look at the paintings at Rosenborg. You can just about make them out.

The Nobel medals that hid in a jar of yellow liquid

Niels Bohr Institute · Copenhagen · April 1940

The Danish physicist Niels Bohr — one of the founders of quantum mechanics — had two gold Nobel Prize medals sitting on his desk in April 1940. One was his own. One had been quietly entrusted to him by his friend Max von Laue in Germany, because von Laue could not safely keep it at home any more. It was against the law to send gold out of the country. Bohr's Hungarian chemist colleague George de Hevesy came up with a plan that was so audacious it worked. He dropped both medals into a small jar of aqua regia — a bright yellow acid that dissolves gold — and left the jar on a lab shelf between two other unlabelled bottles. The soldiers who inspected the lab that spring walked past it, twice, without a second glance. It looked like any other chemistry-lab liquid. The gold sat dissolved in it, invisible, for the rest of the war. Afterwards, de Hevesy carefully precipitated the gold back out of the liquid, sent the powder to Stockholm, and had the medals re-struck. They exist now for the second time. It is the most dramatic thing chemistry has ever done for a friend.

Told to the little one · The Sandman with two umbrellas

Ole Lukøje · Andersen · 1841

There is a small man in Denmark called Ole Lukøje, which means Ole Shut-Eye. Every night he tiptoes into children's bedrooms carrying two umbrellas. Over children who have been kind that day he opens the bright umbrella — the one painted with pictures — and they dream in colours all night. Over children who have been unkind he opens the plain black one, and they sleep with no dreams at all. If she likes, ask her tonight which umbrella she'd like Ole to bring. She has been very kind today. He will already know.
