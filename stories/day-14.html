Day 14 · Sun 11 Oct · Geroldsee → the Salzkammergut

Stories, myths & legends

A lake full of the bones of a giant, a village so old it was mining salt in 1500 BC, a bronze-age miner found in a salt shaft in 1734 that the villagers politely re-buried, and — memorably — a whole Chinese copy of Hallstatt built in 2012.

The bones in Eibsee

Eibsee · below Zugspitze

Eibsee is a small clear alpine lake with eight little islands scattered across it. The scientific version: the lake formed when a massive rockslide came off the Zugspitze about 3,700 years ago, and the islands are the biggest boulders. The Bavarian version: the islands are bones — a giant fell asleep at the foot of the Zugspitze and was crushed under the rockslide, and the eight islands are what still shows of him. Boat-hire operators are careful never to moor to the islands, "so as not to disturb him". They will lend you a boat and let you row wherever you like otherwise.

The Man in Salt

Hallstatt salt mine · found 1734

In 1734 the salt miners of Hallstatt broke through into an ancient shaft and found a body perfectly preserved in the salt — a Bronze Age miner from around 1000 BC, still wearing his leather clothing, his tools next to him. The villagers, disturbed by his condition (which they took as unnatural), had the parish priest re-bury him in the churchyard within a week, without study. He was one of the great archaeological losses of the 18th century. The mine has since yielded thousands of Bronze Age tools and clothing fragments — but not the Man in Salt himself. He is still under the church.

Hallstatt's Chinese twin

Hallstatt, China · Boluo, Guangdong · 2012

In 2011 the villagers of Hallstatt noticed unusual visitors from China — photographers, surveyors, measuring tapes. In 2012 the Chinese property developer Minmetals opened a copy of Hallstatt, in Boluo, Guangdong, to the metre: the same church, the same lake, the same fountain, the same pastel houses. They had not asked permission. The mayor of the Austrian Hallstatt flew out for the opening, was appalled and then charmed, and now the two towns have a formal sister-city agreement. There are two Hallstatts. The one you are in is the older.

Franz Joseph was born of salt

Bad Ischl · Kaiservilla

The Archduchess Sophie, mother of Emperor Franz Joseph, had trouble conceiving for years. On her doctor's advice she took the salt-water baths at Bad Ischl. She conceived within the year, and Franz Joseph was born in 1830 — the first of three Habsburg sons she called her "salt princes". The imperial family kept a summer villa at Bad Ischl for the rest of the Austro-Hungarian Empire's existence. It is not too grand to say that the empire, its final decades, ran on Bad Ischl salt. Sit at Konditorei Zauner today and think of it.

Geroldsee at first light

Geroldsee · below Karwendel

The small alpine lake at Gerold is famous for a reflection: the Karwendel range mirrored perfectly on still water at dawn, framed by three wooden hay-huts on the shore. Local farmers say the huts are older than the lake — that the lake was made when a mountain-spirit was so struck by the wooden huts he flooded the meadow to be able to look at them from underneath. On misty mornings the huts float on the water and you can see why he did it.

Told to the little one · The Alpine flowers and the queen of the mountains

Alpine folklore

The queen of the mountains was said to keep a garden on the highest peak, where the finest flowers grew — edelweiss, gentians, alpine roses. She threw a flower down the mountain every time a child was born in a valley below. Children who find an edelweiss on a walk are said to have been remembered by her. Do not pick them today (they are protected), but point out any small alpine flowers to her, and tell her the queen sent them.
