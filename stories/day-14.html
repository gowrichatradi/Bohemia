Day 14 · Sun 11 Oct · Geroldsee → the Salzkammergut

Stories, myths & legends

A wooden staircase pulled out of a salt mine in 2002 that turned out to be 3,300 years old, in mint condition, with a shape identical to every hay-barn staircase in the valley today. A village so beautiful that a Chinese property developer secretly measured every building and rebuilt the whole thing, to the millimetre, in Guangdong. And a lake with eight small stone islands that are, according to the locals, the toes of a sleeping giant.

The wooden pipeline older than every country in Europe

Soleleitung · Hallstatt → Ebensee · built 1607

The salt of Hallstatt is not dug out as rocks. It is dissolved into water inside the mountain and pumped out as hot brine. In 1607 an engineer named Hans Kalss designed a pipeline to carry that brine down out of the mountain to the wood-fired boiling houses forty kilometres away at Ebensee. He built the pipeline out of long hollowed-out pine trunks, laid end to end, sealed at the joins with a mixture of resin and beeswax. There are thirteen thousand of them in a row. The whole thing runs downhill by gravity — no pumps, no engines, just careful surveying and slope. It was, at the time, the longest industrial pipeline in the world. And here is the astonishing part. It is still running. It has been carrying salt-brine down that same mountain, in wooden trunks, for over four hundred years — older than the United States, older than the piano, older than Newton's laws of motion. Sections have been replaced hollow log by hollow log as they wear out, exactly like a Viking longship, so the pipeline is at once the oldest and newest of its kind. On a quiet day you can walk along it in the forest and put your ear against the wood. It gurgles.

The oldest wooden staircase in Europe

Hallstatt salt mine · found 2002 · dated 1344 BC

In 2002, salt miners deep inside the Hallstatt mountain broke through into a section of tunnel that had been closed off since the Bronze Age, and their headlamps landed on a wooden staircase. It was pristine. Every step, every peg, every rung. The salt had preserved it perfectly, the way it had preserved the Man in Salt three centuries earlier. When archaeologists carried the pieces down the mountain and dendrochronologists dated the tree rings in the wood, they got a number that stopped them: 1344 BC. This staircase was three thousand three hundred years old. It was built more than a thousand years before the birth of Rome. It is now the oldest wooden staircase known to exist anywhere in Europe. And here is the part that will not let you go — the design is almost identical to the wooden staircase at the back of every hay-barn in the Salzkammergut today. The fastest solution to "how do you get up and down a wooden slope" has not changed in three thousand years. The miners who built it were solving the same problem, in the same material, in exactly the same shape, as the farmer in the valley below you is solving right now.

Hallstatt in China

Boluo · Guangdong · unveiled 2012

In 2011 the villagers of Hallstatt began to notice small groups of Chinese visitors with tape measures, cameras on tripods, and clipboards. They were not being rude. They were quietly measuring every façade, every window, every fountain, every cobble. A year later, in 2012, in Boluo county in Guangdong province, a Chinese metals conglomerate opened a full-scale copy of Hallstatt — church, tower, lake, houses, all built to the millimetre from those measurements. They had not asked permission. The mayor of the original Hallstatt flew out, was outraged, then charmed, and signed a formal sister-city agreement on the spot. There are now two Hallstatts on Earth. The one you are standing in is the older, but the copy is younger and slightly cleaner. Both call themselves the real one. Both are.

The empress with the ankle-length hair

Empress Elisabeth · "Sisi" · Bad Ischl · Kaiservilla

Empress Elisabeth of Austria — known everywhere as Sisi — summered every year at Bad Ischl in a low buttermilk-yellow villa called the Kaiservilla. She was extraordinarily beautiful and extraordinarily particular. Her hair reached past her ankles when it was let down. Washing it took a full day and required a mixture of egg yolks and cognac; her hairdresser Fanny, a former circus rider, would carry the fallen hairs to the empress on a silver tray, and Sisi would count them, unhappy at every one. Sisi did all her own gymnastics. Every palace she lived in had rings, ropes and parallel bars installed for her; she trained for hours a day and could balance on a horse standing up. She wrote poetry in secret and left it in her jewellery box, sealed, to be opened only sixty years after her death. It was opened. It was published. It was very good. The Kaiservilla is still exactly as she left it — her writing desk, her books, the small English saddle by the door, and a hair brush on the dresser that has never been touched.

The lake made of a giant's bones

Eibsee · below Zugspitze · formed c. 1700 BC

The Eibsee is a small clear alpine lake at the foot of the Zugspitze, and scattered across it are eight tiny wooded islands. The scientific version of them is that a massive landslide came off the Zugspitze around 3,700 years ago, hit the valley floor at speed, blocked the streams, formed the lake, and left the biggest boulders sticking above the water. The Bavarian version is more interesting. A giant lay down at the foot of the Zugspitze to sleep and never woke up. The rockslide buried him. The eight islands are the last eight parts of him that were tall enough to keep showing above the water — knees, shoulders, ribs, forehead. Boat- hire operators are careful never to moor a boat directly to one of the islands. It would be rude. They will let you row anywhere else.

Told to the little one · The queen of the mountains and her garden

Alpine folklore

The queen of the mountains lives on the highest peak. Nobody ever sees her, but you can see her garden — up there where the air is thin — and she grows the most beautiful flowers in the world. Every time a baby is born in a valley below, she picks one small alpine flower and throws it down the mountain, and it lands somewhere very hard to find. Children who spot a little blue gentian or a tiny edelweiss on their walk have been thought about by her, personally. Don't pick the flowers — they are protected — but point them out and tell her one is hers.
