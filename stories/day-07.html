Day 7 · Sun 4 Oct · Svolvær & Henningsvær

Stories, myths & legends

A village built on skerries, a stone gate the devil threw in a temper, a football pitch the kraken keeps its eye on, and a fish factory where the ghosts of a hundred years of cod politely stay out of the way of art.

The kraken's goal

Henningsvær football pitch

The pitch sits on a small skerry between two islands — grass on rock, sea on all four sides. In old fishermen's stories every ball that goes into the sea is a gift to the kraken, the great squid-monster of the deep, and every goal scored on the pitch is answered by a goal in the kraken's own game far below. When the sea foams at the goal-line without wind, that's the kraken's team celebrating. Nobody has claimed the balls back. There are said to be hundreds down there, a slow accumulation of the game.

Djevelporten — the devil's gate

Djevelporten · above Svolvær

Two great boulders lean against each other above Svolvær, forming a natural arch you can climb through. Local legend says the devil once challenged a Lofoten fisherman to a wager and lost — the fisherman was too clever, or the devil was drunk, versions vary. In a rage the devil hurled two mountains at Svolvær to bury the village. They landed short, wedged into each other, and stopped. The village calls the arch Djevelporten and children play under it in summer, unafraid, because the devil clearly missed.

The Trollfjord's mimicking oar

Trollfjord · Raftsundet

Retold here because it fits the day: rowing alone into the Trollfjord, fishermen said, you could hear a second oar-stroke behind you — a beat late. Row faster, and it kept up. Stop, and it stopped. This is a troll copying you, or an echo, or your own paranoia; the fjord is 100m wide and echoes cleanly. The remedy in any case was the same: row in with another boat and the troll got confused about who to copy.

The KaviarFactory and the fish critics

KaviarFactory · art gallery in a former caviar plant

The gallery is in a stark white building that used to process cod roe for caviar. The founders — collectors Venke and Rolf Hoff — kept the industrial name. Asked why they chose this exact building, Rolf has said, deadpan, that "the ghosts of a hundred million fish are the best art critics in Norway: they've seen everything and they can't be bought." Whether he means this or is joking is deliberately unclear. The gallery is worth an hour.

Trevarefabrikken — the wood remembers

Trevarefabrikken · Henningsvær

A former wooden fish-drying factory, now a cultural centre with a sauna and coffee. The wooden walls are original. Locals will tell you the walls still smell faintly of cod on humid days — a hundred years of fish oil in the timber, which is chemistry. The story that came before the chemistry: that timber which has held fish takes on the memory of the fish, and any building made of it is watched over by the fish that were there. Trevarefabrikken is, by that logic, one of the best-protected buildings on the coast.

Told to the little one · The Sea King's court

Norse folklore

Under the sea lives the Sea King, in a palace of shell and coral. He has a daughter, a great feast every winter, and a fondness for children who eat their fish politely at the top. Any child in Lofoten who eats all of their evening cod is said to be invited (in dreams) to the Sea King's next feast. The Sea King has no time for children who complain about bones.
