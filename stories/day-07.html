Day 7 · Sun 4 Oct · Svolvær & Henningsvær

Stories, myths & legends

A monster so big the entire Norwegian navy has been warned about it in an official report — twice. A stone arch two arguing giants left behind on the ridge. A football pitch on a rock out in the sea. And an ordinary Lofoten woman who, in a boat with her husband, hooked one of the largest halibut ever caught by hand, and had to wait three days before it stopped fighting.

The Kraken that made it into naval reports

Lofoten waters · reported 1752, 1893, 1930s

Everyone thinks the Kraken is a fairy tale until they read the documents. In 1752 the Bishop of Bergen, Erik Pontoppidan, wrote a serious natural history in which he described sailors' reports of a sea-creature "one and a half English miles across", its back appearing above the surface like an island, its arms tall as ship masts. He was not writing a story. He was compiling testimony. The Norwegian navy has since had officers file at least two formal sightings of an enormous unidentified sea-animal off the Lofoten coast — one in the 1890s, one in the 1930s. Modern zoologists think it was almost certainly a giant squid, which really does exist and really does grow to at least thirteen metres. The word "kraken" is Norwegian. It is one of very few Norwegian words to have entered every language on Earth.

The football pitch in the middle of the sea

Henningsvær Stadion · population of Henningsvær: 500

Somebody in this fishing village of five hundred people decided they needed a full-sized football pitch. There was no flat land. There is never any flat land in Lofoten. So they built one on a rocky islet a hundred metres offshore, poured concrete over the rock, laid a green artificial surface on top, and made the floodlights face away from the fish drying racks. There are no stands. Balls that go over the touchline go into the North Atlantic. It has become one of the most photographed football grounds on Earth — an aerial shot of a green rectangle on a black rock in a slate-blue sea, with mountains behind it, has appeared in FIFA calendars for a decade. The village team plays here on Sunday afternoons. Anybody can watch. Bring warm clothes.

The gate two giants built by mistake

Djevelporten · above Svolvær

Two great boulders lean against each other on the ridge above Svolvær, forming a natural stone arch you can walk under. Very brave climbers even jump the two-metre gap between the tops of them, six hundred metres straight above the village, for a photograph. Geology's version is that a slow rockfall during the last ice age sent two boulders sliding down the mountain together and they wedged into each other where they stopped. Lofoten's older version is that two giants were walking along the ridge carrying rocks, arguing about whose rock was bigger, and dropped them at exactly the same moment. Neither would pick his up until the other did. The sun came up before either would give in. Giants turn to stone in the sun. The rocks are still there. So, presumably, are the giants. Children play under it all summer.

The woman and the halibut of Skrova

Off Skrova · 1961 · 220 kg

A story every Skrova household knows. In the early 1960s a woman named Marta went out in an open boat with her husband to fish halibut on a summer evening. Something took her hook and did not come up. Halibut are flatfish that can grow to enormous size and simply refuse to be moved — they lie on the bottom and wait. Marta and her husband could not haul it. They also could not cut the line, because they needed the hook. So they sat there, in shifts, holding the line, sleeping in turns. On the third day the halibut got tired and drifted up. It was over two metres long and weighed more than she did. She caught bigger fish afterwards. But that one she told for the rest of her life.

The wood that remembers the fish

Trevarefabrikken · Henningsvær

The old wooden factory at Trevarefabrikken has been many things — fish-drying works, boat-hall, cultural centre with a sauna in a floating cabin outside — but it has always been the same wood. Locals say if you sit inside on a damp afternoon you can still smell cod. This is chemistry: a century of fish oil has soaked into the timber and does not come out. The older story is that wood which has held fish holds the memory of the fish, and any building made of it is quietly watched over by every animal that was ever hung in it. Trevarefabrikken is, if you believe the older story, one of the most closely observed buildings in Norway. Nothing bad ever happens there. Sit and have a coffee.

Told to the little one · The Sea King's dinner party

Norse folklore

Under the sea lives the Sea King, in a palace built of pink coral and mother-of-pearl. He has a daughter with hair the colour of seaweed and a fondness for children who eat their fish neatly. Every winter he throws a great dinner party under the ice for all the children who finished their supper politely. You get an invitation in a dream. His only rule is that nobody at the table is allowed to complain about the bones.
