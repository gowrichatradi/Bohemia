Day 7 · Sun 4 Oct · Aurora day — Polar Park or Abisko

Stories, myths & legends

Every culture that lives under the aurora has a story for it, and most of them warn you against the same thing: don't wave, don't whistle, don't call. Under the Torneträsk sky tonight you are inside a set of overlapping folk-belief systems — Sámi, Finnish, Norwegian, and (across the border) Swedish — each with a different name for the same green fire.

The Sámi aurora — guovssahasat

Northern Sámi · "the lights you can hear"

In Northern Sámi the aurora is guovssahasat, roughly "the lights you can hear". Old belief is that the lights are spirits of the dead — you must not wave at them, whistle at them, or draw their attention with a bright light or a raised voice, because they will come down and take you. A Sámi guide in the 1930s refused to use a camera flash for exactly this reason. Take the taboo as a story to tell the little one; leave the camera on.

The Finnish fire-fox

Revontulet · "fox fires"

The Finnish word for the aurora is revontulet — literally "fox fires". The old story is that a giant Arctic fox runs across the tundra with such speed that its tail brushes the snow and sends sparks into the sky. Photographers coming to Finnish Lapland often use fox-fire as a brand name; it comes from this. In Abisko the mountain to the west is called Nuolja, "the fox's cape" in Sámi — the same fox, in a different language.

The Sky Station's rain shadow

Why Abisko has clear nights when Narvik has cloud

The reason you drove east is not folklore — it's meteorology. The Norwegian coastal mountains catch the Atlantic weather and dump it as rain and snow on the west side; by the time the air reaches Torneträsk it has almost nothing left to give. Abisko has 250 clear-sky nights a year while Narvik, 60 km west, has fewer than 100. NASA has used this rain-shadow site for aurora research since the 1960s.

The ghost destroyers of Rombaksbotn

Second Battle of Narvik · 13 April 1940

The fjord finger running east from Narvik holds the wrecks of nine German destroyers sunk or scuttled on one day in April 1940 — one of the worst days in Kriegsmarine history. Local fishermen say the wrecks still call: sonar returns from the fjord include mechanical clicks nobody has ever explained, and the winter fog rolls in over the water in the shape of a ship. Both stories are exaggerated, and both stories are told to every child who grows up in Narvik.

The Icehotel — a story that builds itself every winter

Jukkasjärvi · 1989 onwards

The Icehotel started as an art installation by Yngve Bergqvist in 1989 — a single igloo of art on the frozen Torne river. Every winter since, it is rebuilt with rooms carved by different international artists from scratch. The rooms melt back into the river every May. There is no permanent hotel; there are 34 winters of hotels, each one lost. If you push into Jukkasjärvi tomorrow morning, the Ice Chapel next door is where 200 couples a year marry inside a room that will be gone by June.

Told to the little one · The fox who painted the sky

A Finnish story · for the aurora night

There was once a fox who lived very far north, and he loved to run across the snow. One night he ran so fast that his tail caught in the snow and swept it up, and the snow flew up into the sky and stuck there, and it turned green and pink and violet. And every winter after that, whenever the fox is running his very fastest across the snow — she can look up and see where his tail has been. The lights in the sky are his footprints. That is why we call them fox-fires.
