Day 16 · Tue 13 Oct · Prague — the old city

Stories, myths & legends

Prague is a city that runs on stories — a clay giant sleeping in an attic, a clockmaker blinded so he couldn't build another clock, a bridge held together with eggs, a saint whose stars appeared over the water when they threw him in. Every stone has a version.

The Golem in the attic

Old New Synagogue · Rabbi Loew · 1580

Rabbi Loew of Prague made a giant out of clay from the banks of the Vltava, wrote the Hebrew word emet (truth) on its forehead, and brought it to life to protect the Jewish quarter. He deactivated it every Sabbath by rubbing out the first letter, leaving met (dead). The Golem worked until it grew violent, at which point Loew put it to rest permanently — in the attic of the Old New Synagogue, where it is said to still be. The attic has been forbidden to visitors since. A Nazi soldier who reportedly went up in 1941 to investigate did not come back down. This is the version. There is a plaque nearby. There is not, officially, a Golem.

The blinded clockmaker

Orloj · Astronomical Clock · 1410

Prague's Astronomical Clock was, at 600 years old, the most sophisticated clock in the world when built. The story: Master Hanuš, credited with completing it around 1490, was so admired that the Prague town councillors — fearing he would build an even better one for another city — had him blinded. He crawled up into the clock tower, put his hand into the mechanism, and stopped it. It took the city a hundred years to get it running again. Modern historians say Hanuš probably didn't exist. The story doesn't care. It's a good story.

Charles Bridge and the village that sent boiled eggs

Charles Bridge · 1357

When Charles IV was building the bridge, engineers advised him to strengthen the mortar with egg whites — a real medieval technique. The king ordered every village in Bohemia to send eggs. Most sent raw eggs. The village of Velvary, taking no chances that their eggs would break on the way, hard-boiled the entire consignment. The people of Velvary have been the subject of Czech jokes ever since. The bridge still stands.

The five stars of St John of Nepomuk

Charles Bridge · Statue 8 (north side)

In 1393, King Wenceslas IV had his queen's confessor, Father Jan of Nepomuk, thrown from Charles Bridge into the Vltava for refusing to reveal what she had said in confession. As his body sank, five stars appeared over the water at the spot where he went in — one for each letter of the Latin word tacui, "I was silent". He was later made a saint. His statue on the bridge shows a halo of five stars, and if you touch the bronze plaque at its base you are promised to return to Prague. Half the plaque is polished to a mirror by tourists. Half the tourists come back.

Kafka burned his own manuscripts

Franz Kafka · 1883–1924

Kafka worked full-time as an insurance clerk at the Workmen's Accident Insurance Institute — a job he called his "day-time nightmare". He wrote at night, and he burned most of what he wrote. He estimated later, in a letter, that he had destroyed about 90% of his own work. On his deathbed he asked his best friend, Max Brod, to burn the rest, unread. Brod disobeyed. Everything Kafka is now famous for — The Trial, The Castle, Amerika — was published against Kafka's explicit wishes by a man breaking a promise to a dying friend. The bronze Kafka statue in Josefov is the price of that betrayal.

Twelve layers of graves

Old Jewish Cemetery · Josefov · 15th–18th c.

The Old Jewish Cemetery holds around 12,000 visible headstones, but an estimated 100,000 people are buried in it — because the Jewish community was forbidden from expanding the cemetery's boundary, and were required by faith not to disturb existing graves. So they added earth on top and buried again. And again. There are twelve layers of graves stacked. That is why the headstones lean, and why the ground rises in the middle. Each visible stone is often the topmost of a stack of ancestors.

Told to the little one · The princess and the pea

Andersen · but told all over Central Europe

A prince wanted to marry a real princess. His mother the queen tested one visiting girl by putting a single pea under twenty mattresses and twenty featherbeds, then having her sleep on the pile. In the morning the girl complained she'd hardly slept — something hard had bruised her all night. Only a real princess would feel a pea through twenty mattresses. Reader, they married. The moral your daughter will draw is her own.
