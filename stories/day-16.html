Day 16 · Tue 13 Oct · Prague — the old city

Stories, myths & legends

A clay giant asleep in the beams of a synagogue attic where nobody is allowed up. A six-hundred-year-old clock that shows you where the sun is in the sky, in real time, at a glance. A bridge held together with the boiled eggs of an entire village. A cemetery where the ground rises in the middle because there are twelve layers of graves stacked underneath. And a writer whose entire fame is a broken promise.

The clay giant in the attic

The Golem · Old New Synagogue · Rabbi Loew · 1580

Every child in Prague knows this one. In the 1580s Rabbi Judah Loew, chief rabbi of Prague, walked down to the banks of the Vltava at midnight with his son-in-law and his best student and, with clay from the river, shaped a giant seven feet tall. They walked around it seven times reciting the letters of the Hebrew alphabet. Then Loew placed a small piece of parchment under its tongue with a single Hebrew word: emet, meaning truth. The giant sat up. His name was Josef. He was strong beyond measure. He was mute. Loew put him to work watching over the Jewish quarter and, on the Sabbath, rested him by taking the parchment out. The Golem worked for years. Eventually the community felt safe enough that he was not needed any more, so Loew wrapped him gently in an old prayer shawl, and carried him up into the attic of the Old New Synagogue in the middle of Prague, where he laid him down to sleep in case the community should ever need him again. The attic exists. It is still there. Nobody is allowed up. Prague children have been told for four hundred years that he is still asleep in the beams, and that if the city ever really needs him he will simply wake up. The synagogue is the oldest still-active synagogue in Europe. He has not been called on. Nobody is in a hurry.

The clock that knows where the sun is

Orloj · Astronomical Clock · installed 1410

Prague's Astronomical Clock is the third-oldest working clock in the world and, when it was built in 1410, was the most sophisticated machine in Europe. It does not just tell the time. It tells the position of the sun in the sky as you look at it. It tells the phase of the moon and where in the sky the moon currently is. It tells the day of the Gregorian calendar and the day of the old Bohemian calendar in parallel. It tells the current zodiac sign, the current saint, the length of the day, the length of the night, and the sunrise and sunset — all at a glance, all on one dial, all six hundred years ago, before anyone had heard of a telescope. On the hour, twelve small wooden apostles come out of two windows above the dial and parade past, one at a time, while the whole square looks up. Stand in front of it at five to the hour. Notice how many different centuries you are looking at at once.

The village of the hard-boiled eggs

Charles Bridge · 1357 · Velvary

When Charles IV was building Charles Bridge in the fourteenth century, his engineers advised him that eggs mixed into the mortar would make it stronger. This is a real medieval technique. He sent messengers to every village in Bohemia demanding a cartload of eggs. Most villages sent eggs. The village of Velvary, worried the eggs would break on the road, hard-boiled the entire consignment before loading them. The king reportedly opened one of the Velvary crates on arrival and stood staring at a boiled egg for a long time. He did not punish the village. He simply told the story at court, and the story escaped, and Velvary has been the subject of affectionate Czech jokes for six hundred and seventy years. The bridge, whether the eggs were runny or firm, is still standing. Its stones survived one of the most powerful floods in European history in 2002 with almost no damage.

The saint who kept a secret and got five stars

Charles Bridge · Statue 8 · Jan of Nepomuk · 1393

Jan of Nepomuk was a real priest, and in the year 1393 he was confessor to the queen of Bohemia. The king, an anxious man, badly wanted to know what his wife was telling her priest during confession. He asked Jan. Jan, whose whole training taught him that a confession is a sealed thing forever, politely refused. The king asked again. Jan refused again. He would not betray a single word. That night, according to the Prague tradition, five stars appeared in the sky above the Vltava — one for each letter of the Latin word tacui, I was silent. Nepomuk was made a saint centuries later, and his statue is now the eighth on Charles Bridge, wearing a golden halo of exactly five stars. If you rub the bronze plaque at his feet, gently, you are promised a return to Prague. Half the tourists on the bridge are doing it as you walk past. The plaque is polished mirror- bright by three centuries of hands.

The writer who tried to burn everything

Franz Kafka · 1883–1924

Kafka worked full-time as a clerk at the Bohemian Workmen's Accident Insurance Institute. He was excellent at his job. He wrote fire-safety reports so clear that other insurance companies studied them. He came home in the evenings and wrote his novels at night on a small table in a rented room. He hated most of what he wrote. He burned about ninety percent of it in the fireplace. On his deathbed in a sanatorium outside Vienna, dying of tuberculosis at forty, he wrote a letter to his closest friend Max Brod asking him, please, to burn everything else — unread. Brod refused. Every book that made Kafka the most famous writer of the twentieth century — The Trial, The Castle, Amerika — was published against Kafka's dying wish. Everything he is admired for is a broken promise. The bronze statue in Josefov, the man riding on the shoulders of a hollow coat, is the price Brod paid, and it looks appropriately uneasy.

The cemetery that had to grow upwards

Old Jewish Cemetery · Josefov · 15th–18th centuries

Walk into the Old Jewish Cemetery and you will see twelve thousand headstones leaning at all angles in an area the size of a large courtyard, and the ground rises so much in the middle that it forms a small hill. Both of these things exist for the same reason. The city forbade the Jewish community from ever expanding the cemetery's boundary. Their faith forbade them from ever removing a body once buried. So over three hundred years, when they ran out of space, they simply brought in new earth and buried on top. Then again. Then again. There are twelve layers of graves under your feet at the highest point. Every visible headstone is often only the topmost marker of a stack of ancestors, one leaning into another, the whole cemetery slowly pushing itself upward like a memory too heavy to be flat.

Told to the little one · The princess who felt a pea

Andersen · told all over Central Europe

A prince wanted to marry a real princess. Not just any princess — a real one. So many girls came to the palace claiming to be princesses that no one could tell any more. His mother the queen came up with a plan. She invited the next visiting girl to spend the night. Then she took a tiny green pea, one pea, and put it on the girl's bed. On top of the pea she piled twenty mattresses. On top of those, twenty featherbeds. In the morning she asked the girl how she'd slept. Terribly, said the girl. Something hard had bruised her all night. Only a real princess could feel a pea through twenty mattresses and twenty featherbeds. Reader, they got married. Ask her, at bedtime, if she thinks she'd feel a pea.
