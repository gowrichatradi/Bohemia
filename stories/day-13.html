Day 13 · Sat 10 Oct · Bergen → Munich → Neuschwanstein

Stories, myths & legends

A king who built a fairy-tale castle and died in a lake with no water in his lungs. A wooden Christ who wept in a farmhouse. A ghost swan on Alpsee. And the throne room that never got a throne.

Ludwig II, the swan king

Neuschwanstein · Ludwig II · d. 1886

Ludwig II of Bavaria — the Fairy-tale King — spent his personal fortune building three castles in the Alps and lived in Neuschwanstein for only 172 days before he was declared insane and deposed. Three days later he was found floating in Lake Starnberg alongside his psychiatrist. The autopsy noted no water in his lungs. He was 40. Locals still say his ghost crosses the Alpsee on a swan-boat on the anniversary of his death (13 June), and that on quiet autumn nights, if the light is right, you can see the wake he leaves. There is a large modern tourism industry built around a man who was quite unwell and probably murdered — you are contributing to it. He would have loved that.

The Christ who wept in Steingaden

Wieskirche · pilgrimage church · 1745

A wooden statue of the Scourged Christ, made in 1738 for a Good Friday procession, was retired to a farmhouse attic in Steingaden. In 1738 a farmer's wife, Maria Lory, went to pray in front of it and saw real tears on the wooden face. Word travelled. Pilgrims arrived by the thousands. The Wieskirche was built on the exact spot in 1745, in a rococo style so ornate the ceiling almost lifts off. The statue is still there, in the middle of the altar. Whether it wept — and it did, according to Maria Lory, whom no one had reason to doubt — is a matter you can decide when you're standing in front of it.

The maiden of Alpsee

Alpsee · below the castles

A German version of the Lorelei: a fair-haired maiden was said to sit on a rock in Alpsee at dawn, combing her hair with a comb of gold. Fishermen who saw her had good catches. Fishermen who tried to steal the comb — and there was always one every generation — never made it home. The story was told to Ludwig as a boy at Hohenschwangau, and he asked whether the maiden was real. His mother, Queen Marie, is said to have replied, "she is exactly as real as you need her to be." He built a lake beneath his own bedroom window at Neuschwanstein, in case.

The throne room without a throne

Neuschwanstein · unfinished

Only 14 of Neuschwanstein's planned 200 rooms were finished when Ludwig died. The great throne room was completed except for the throne itself, which had been commissioned but never delivered. It stands today with a broad marble apse where the throne should be — a dais under a golden canopy, and nothing on it. The castle staff say some visitors see something on the dais briefly out of the corner of the eye. The building was never consecrated. You can decide whether that matters.

Lechfall's green water

Lechfall · Füssen

The Lech river runs bright turquoise-green through Füssen, coloured by finely-ground rock flour from the alpine limestone. The old folktale for children was that a mermaid lived in the pool below the falls and washed her green hair every morning, and the colour ran into the water. Small versions of the mermaid still sit as sculptures around Füssen. Rock flour is the truth, but the mermaid is nicer, and does not require you to explain what "rock flour" is to a three-year-old.

Told to the little one · The swan prince

Bavarian folklore · via Wagner

A prince was under an enchantment that turned him into a swan every day, and back to a prince every night. He fell in love with a princess. She was not to ask his name — that would break the spell. She asked. The swan flew away. Wagner turned this story into Lohengrin, an opera Ludwig II watched over and over, and it is why every wall in Neuschwanstein has a swan on it. Point at the swan cushions and tell her the whole castle is by a boy who really liked swans. That is true.
