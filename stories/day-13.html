Day 13 · Sat 10 Oct · Bergen → Munich → Neuschwanstein

Stories, myths & legends

A king who spent his fortune building fairy-tale castles in his own mountains, watched Wagner operas alone at three in the morning, and became — a century after his death — the model for the Disney logo. A wooden statue in a farmhouse attic that wept real tears one afternoon and started a pilgrimage that built a whole rococo cathedral in a field. And a throne room built for a throne that never arrived.

The king who lived like a fairy tale

Ludwig II of Bavaria · 1845–1886

Ludwig II of Bavaria became king at eighteen — six foot four inches tall, shy to the point of illness, and completely in love with the operas of Richard Wagner. He hated politics. He hated state banquets. He hated crowds. What he wanted, more than anything, was to build fairy-tale castles in his mountains and live inside them. So he did. He spent his personal fortune on three of them — Neuschwanstein, Linderhof, and Herrenchiemsee — and put Bavaria on the world map for a kind of architecture that had never existed before. He watched Wagner's operas by himself, at three in the morning, in his own empty theatre. He built an artificial grotto lit by electric lights, which was one of the first uses of electric lighting in Bavaria. He wrote letters to King Louis XIV of France, dead for two hundred years, as if the two of them were pen pals. His ministers thought he was impossible. Every child in Bavaria thinks he was magnificent. The castle you are about to visit is the reason the entire Disney logo exists — Walt Disney saw a picture of Neuschwanstein and rebuilt it, ninety years later, as the Sleeping Beauty Castle in Anaheim. Ludwig would have loved that. It is his building, exported into every childhood on Earth.

The wooden Christ that wept in a farmhouse

Wieskirche · Steingaden · pilgrimage since 1738

In 1738 a farmer's wife named Maria Lory was cleaning a small wooden figure of Christ in the attic of her farmhouse near Steingaden. The statue had been put away because it was too harrowing to look at during processions. Kneeling in front of it, alone, on the fourteenth of June, she noticed water on the wooden face. She wiped it. It came back. She called her husband. He saw it too. Word spread. Farmers came from neighbouring valleys. Then from the next region. Then from all of Bavaria. Within seven years the pilgrimage was so large that an entire rococo pilgrimage church had to be built in the field, around the statue, in a style so lavishly gold and pale-blue that its ceiling seems to lift when you walk in. The statue is still there, in the middle of the altar. It is a UNESCO site. It stands in a meadow next to some cows. And it is entirely because Maria Lory told the truth on a very ordinary Tuesday.

The maiden of Alpsee

Alpsee · below Hohenschwangau

The lake below the castles has an old Bavarian story attached to it, and Ludwig grew up hearing it. A fair-haired maiden was said to sit on a certain rock at dawn combing her hair with a comb of gold. Fishermen who spotted her and left her alone had extraordinary catches all season. Fishermen who tried to keep her comb for themselves found their nets came up empty until they gave it back. Ludwig asked his mother, Queen Marie, whether the maiden was real. She is said to have answered, gently: She is exactly as real as you need her to be. He remembered the line the rest of his life. When he built Neuschwanstein he made sure there was a small artificial pond visible from his own bedroom window. Just in case.

The throne room with no throne

Neuschwanstein · unfinished at Ludwig's death

Ludwig died with only fourteen of the two hundred rooms of Neuschwanstein finished. The throne room was one of the fourteen — a golden Byzantine hall two storeys high, dome painted with saints, marble floor with a jewelled mosaic — and the throne that was to sit at its far end had been commissioned from a goldsmith in Munich but was not delivered before he died. It never has been. Walk into the throne room today and the whole space aims itself at a low marble dais under a golden canopy — a dais with nothing on it. The gap in the middle of the room is more affecting than any throne could ever have been. The castle was never officially consecrated as a royal residence. You can decide, standing there, whether the absence matters.

The green water of the Lech

Lechfall · Füssen

The river Lech runs through Füssen bright turquoise-green — an absurd colour, the colour of a swimming pool with an overactive chemist. The scientific answer is rock flour — a suspension of finely ground alpine limestone in the melt water, which scatters the blue-green wavelengths of light and absorbs the rest. The story that Bavarian parents preferred to tell their children is that a mermaid lives in the pool below the falls and washes her green hair every morning, and the colour runs downstream into Füssen. Small stone mermaids sit along the riverbank. Both explanations are, at three years old, entirely equal in weight.

Told to the little one · The prince who became a swan

Bavarian folktale · Wagner's Lohengrin

A very long time ago, a prince fell under an enchantment. Every morning at sunrise he turned into a great white swan, and every evening at sunset he turned back into a prince. He fell in love with a princess. She promised — she promised! — never to ask his name. But she asked. And in the morning he was a swan again, and this time he flew away and never came back. Ludwig heard this story as a little boy. He liked it so much that he built the whole of Neuschwanstein with swans on every wall, every cushion, every doorknob and every drinking cup. Point out the swans to her. There are hundreds.
