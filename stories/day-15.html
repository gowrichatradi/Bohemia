Day 15 · Mon 12 Oct · Hallstatt → Krumlov → Prague

Stories, myths & legends

A castle where bears still live in the moat because the family claimed descent from bears. A White Lady whose gloves tell you what's coming. An alchemist who may or may not have turned lead into gold. A ballroom whose frescoes come alive at full moon.

Bears in the moat

Český Krumlov Castle · bears since 1707

Since 1707 there have been live brown bears in the dry moat of Krumlov castle. The Rosenberg family, who ruled here for centuries, claimed descent from the Italian Orsini family — orso is Italian for bear — and kept live bears as heraldic proof. The tradition survives. There are two bears in the moat today, well-fed, and a keeper who cares for them like elderly aunts. Locals still call the current pair "the Rosenberg cousins". Look down from the cloak bridge and you'll almost certainly see them dozing.

The White Lady's gloves

Perchta of Rosenberg · d. 1476

Perchta of Rosenberg was a real 15th-century noblewoman, unhappily married and freed only by widowhood. After her death she was said to appear as a White Lady in Rosenberg family properties — Krumlov, Jindřichův Hradec, Třeboň — always to warn. Her gloves told you what was coming. White gloves: good news, usually a birth or a marriage. Black gloves: death in the family. Red gloves: fire. Documented aristocratic sightings continued into the 20th century. The last recorded (in a family diary at Jindřichův Hradec) was 1908; she was wearing white.

Edward Kelley, alchemist

Krumlov castle · 1590s

Edward Kelley, English alchemist and medium, worked for Emperor Rudolf II and later for the lord of Krumlov, Vilém of Rosenberg. He claimed — and Rudolf II claimed on his behalf — to have successfully transmuted lead into gold, once, in a demonstration in front of witnesses. What is documented is that Rudolf ennobled him, gave him estates, and then jailed him when he refused (or failed) to repeat the trick. Kelley died escaping from prison in 1597. The alembic he supposedly used is still (allegedly) somewhere in Krumlov castle. Ask a guide. They will smile.

The frescoes that come alive

Rosenberg Ballroom · Maskensaal · 1748

The Maskensaal at Krumlov is covered in life-size trompe-l'œil frescoes of a masked ball — noblemen, servants, dancers, a magician, a small boy peeking from behind a curtain — painted by Josef Lederer in 1748. Locals have said for two hundred years that on nights of the full moon the painted figures move — a step to the left, a raised glass — always small enough that you're not sure. Curators will not humour this, but they also, quietly, do not schedule night tours on full-moon evenings. It is easier for everyone.

Freistadt and the pot of coins

Freistadt · Austrian border town

Freistadt was a medieval customs town on the salt road between Bohemia and the Danube. The legend goes that every traveller passing through in the Middle Ages had to drop a coin into a stone bowl by the town gate — a kind of toll. When the town's finances ran short the bowl was emptied and counted, and it was always exactly what was needed. The story is that the bowl "knew" the town's need. The bowl still exists in the town museum. Modern accountants dispute it.

Told to the little one · The town musicians

Grimm · Bremen town musicians

An old donkey, an old dog, an old cat, and an old rooster — each abandoned by their owners for being too old to be useful — set out for Bremen to become musicians. On the way they came across a house of robbers, stood on top of each other in the window, and made such a terrible noise that the robbers ran. The animals stayed and lived there ever after. Grimm's fairy tale, told all over Central Europe. Moral: being old and unwanted is a good foundation for a band.
