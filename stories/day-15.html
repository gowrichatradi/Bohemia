Day 15 · Mon 12 Oct · Hallstatt → Krumlov → Prague

Stories, myths & legends

A castle whose moat has been full of live bears since 1707, because the family that lived here believed they were descended from a bear. An open-air theatre in the gardens where six hundred seats rotate on a giant turntable to face the next stage in the trees. An English alchemist who almost made gold once, in front of witnesses, and could never do it again. And a ballroom whose painter finished the room by painting himself into the corner.

The bears in the moat

Český Krumlov Castle · bears since 1707

There are actual live brown bears living in the dry moat of Krumlov Castle. They have been there for over three hundred years. The reason is that the Rosenberg family, who ruled the castle for most of the middle ages, insisted they were descended from the great Italian Orsini family — and orso is Italian for bear. Genealogically it was almost certainly untrue. The Rosenbergs decided to prove it by simply being surrounded by bears at all times: bears on the coat of arms, bears on the tapestries, bears carved into the doors, and, when they moved into Krumlov, bears in the moat. Successive owners have kept it going ever since. There are two bears down there right now, and they have a keeper who fusses over them like elderly aunts. Look over the cloak-bridge and you'll almost certainly see one asleep in a patch of sun. Locals still call them "the Rosenberg cousins".

The theatre that turns instead of the actors

Krumlov Revolving Auditorium · built 1958 · gardens

Deep in the gardens of Krumlov Castle is one of only two of its kind in the world — a large open-air theatre where the audience does the moving. Six hundred and fifty seats are mounted on an enormous circular turntable, and during a performance the whole seating platform rotates, slowly and silently, to face different outdoor stages set in the trees. Scene one: a woodland glade to the north. The seating turns quietly on its axis. Scene two: a stone amphitheatre to the east. Turn again. Scene three: a candlelit lawn under the hornbeams. Instead of building elaborate sets and swapping them behind a curtain, the theatre simply swings the entire audience towards the next place. It was invented in 1958 as a technical experiment, and it is now one of the most beloved summer venues in Europe. Operas from Mozart to Bizet are performed here — three thousand seats over a summer, a hundred thousand people over a decade — all of them being quietly, mechanically, delightedly turned to face the story as it moves through the woods.

The English alchemist who almost made gold

Edward Kelley · Krumlov · 1585–1595

Edward Kelley arrived in Prague in 1584, an English alchemist with long dark hair and a black obsidian mirror through which, he claimed, he could speak to angels. He had come to Bohemia with his partner John Dee — the court astrologer of Queen Elizabeth I — and Rudolf II, the Holy Roman Emperor, was fascinated by both of them. Kelley eventually gave a public demonstration in which a small quantity of mercury was said to be transmuted into a lump of pure gold. The lump was tested by the imperial goldsmith. It was real. Rudolf ennobled Kelley on the spot, gave him estates in Bohemia, gave him a Bohemian wife. He then asked, politely, if he might do it again. Kelley could not — or would not — and for eight years he moved from one castle to another dodging the request with elaborate excuses, alchemy diaries and increasingly obscure Latin. Nobody has ever explained what he actually did that first time. The lump has never been found. Somewhere in the accounts of Krumlov castle, they say, is a receipt for a very small piece of gold. Bearing his signature.

The room where the painter is still watching

Maskensaal · Krumlov · painted 1748 · Josef Lederer

The Masquerade Hall of Krumlov Castle is covered, floor to ceiling, in a single enormous fresco by the Viennese painter Josef Lederer — one hundred and thirty-five life-size figures at a masked ball, painted in six months, with such confidence that they seem to have just this second stopped talking to each other. A magician holds a coin between his fingers. A drummer boy is dressed as a rooster. A woman laughs into her fan. A dwarf carries a tray of silver cups. A small boy peeks out shyly from behind a curtain. That small boy is Lederer's own son. And in the corner behind the harp player, hand on hip, wearing a green coat and a wry expression, watching everyone at the party quietly, is Lederer himself. He is the only person in the room who has been there for the last two hundred and seventy years. Ask the guide to point him out. He is a marvel — the painter who finished a room by walking into it.

The salt road and the bowl that knew

Freistadt · medieval customs town

You drive today across an ancient trade route — the salt road that carried Hallstatt salt north into Bohemia for a thousand years. Freistadt was the customs town in the middle. Every traveller who passed through, in the medieval version of the story, had to drop a coin into a stone bowl beside the town gate. It was less a toll than a courtesy. When the town's coffers ran low the bowl was emptied and counted, and it always turned out to hold exactly the amount that was needed to cover the shortfall. Nobody was ever sure how. There are two competing modern explanations: an honest treasurer with a good sense of timing, or a piece of medieval folk-tax psychology in which the town simply reported whatever the bowl contained. The bowl still exists. It is in the town museum. It is completely unremarkable to look at.

Told to the little one · Four old animals and a house full of robbers

The Bremen Town Musicians · Grimm

There was once an old donkey who was too tired to work, an old dog who was too old to hunt, an old cat who was too old to catch mice, and an old rooster who was too old to crow. Each one had been thrown out by their people. They met on the road and decided to go to Bremen together and become musicians. On the way they came across a warm little house in the woods full of robbers eating dinner. The animals stood on top of each other in front of the window — dog on donkey, cat on dog, rooster on cat — and all began to sing at the same time. The robbers ran into the forest, thinking a monster was coming. The animals moved in and lived there happily. They never got to Bremen. The lesson every Central European child gets from this: old and unwanted is a perfectly good start for a band.
