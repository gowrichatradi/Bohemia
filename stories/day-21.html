Day 21 · Sun 18 Oct · Home

Stories, myths & legends

The long way south. A driverless train whose front window is the best view in the airport. A plane that flies for eight hours along a river of stars that nobody on the ground can see. And, waiting for you when you get home, the oldest continuous stories on Earth — the Dreamtime songlines of the country you are landing in, some of them older than any written history anywhere.

The best seat in the airport

Copenhagen Metro M2 · driverless since 2007

The Copenhagen Metro was one of the first fully driverless city metros in the world. It has been running since 2007 without a single human at the controls of any train. The computers do everything — accelerate, brake, open and close the doors, adjust for the weight of the passengers, keep the right spacing between trains. What that means, delightfully, is that on the M2 there is no cab. The front window of every carriage looks straight down the tunnel. Children figure this out immediately, drag their parents to the very front, and press their faces against the glass. The tunnel lights come at you like a fairground ride. Copenhagen is one of the few cities on Earth that gives the tunnel view to the four- year-olds rather than the drivers. Do that one last time on the way to the plane. It is the cheapest ride at the airport.

The road of stars above Siberia

Northern long-haul routes · pilot folklore

On the polar-adjacent routes that take you south — Copenhagen to Singapore, London to Tokyo — the plane crosses at forty thousand feet over Siberia and the Kazakh steppe, above the weather, above almost every human light on Earth. Long-haul pilots have a private name for the view on a clear night up there. They call it the Milky Way Road. The stars, undimmed by any city glow for thousands of kilometres in any direction, appear so dense that they seem to form a paved surface you could walk on. Between about three and five in the morning local time, if the shades are up and you are on the right side of the plane, you get it. Try to be awake for one hour. See the road. Then sleep. The rest of the flight is nothing.

The suitcase full of scissors and paper

Hans Christian Andersen · his travelling suitcase

Andersen went on many, many long journeys, and he never packed lightly. His travelling suitcase, kept today in the Odense museum, contained on his last trip: a change of clothes, a Bible, a fountain pen, a small notebook, a bundle of folded coloured paper, a pair of very sharp little scissors, a lock of a woman's hair he had loved for forty years without ever telling her, and one dried flower. Wherever he stopped for the night — at a farmhouse, at an inn, at the palaces of kings — he would take out the scissors and the paper and cut a small silhouette for the child of the house: a swan, a windmill, a castle with tiny turrets, a ballerina on tiptoe, sometimes all four in one composition. He gave them away. He signed almost nothing. Over three hundred survive in museums today. He wrote fairytales that changed how the world tells stories to children. But he told friends, at the end of his life, that the little paper cuttings were his best work — because he made them for you, and then gave them to you. Take a small piece of that home with you.

The dust-devils that carry news

Melbourne · Kulin country · willy-willies

In many Aboriginal traditions across southern Australia the little dust-devils that spin across an open paddock at dusk — willy-willies — are not weather. They are spirits. Sometimes ancestors, sometimes messengers, carrying news between one country and another. You do not walk through one. You do not chase one. If one goes past, you give it the same small nod you would give an aunt in the street. Whether or not you believe, there is a decent chance that in your first week home, walking across an oval or past a paddock, one will spin past you. After three weeks in the folklore of old Europe, after fjord giants and clay giants and thrones with no throne, you may find yourself nodding at it before you notice. That is the country you come from, greeting you back.

The songs that are older than every story you just heard

Australia · continuous storytelling for 65,000 years

One last thing. Every story on this trip is old. Some go back to the Vikings. Some go back to the Bronze Age miner in the salt. Some — the Kraken, Ragnarök, the world-serpent — reach back three or four thousand years. All of them are children compared to the stories that are waiting for you at home. Aboriginal Australians have been telling continuous stories, in the same country, for at least sixty-five thousand years. There are songs that describe coastlines that were dry land at the end of the last ice age and have been under the sea ever since — passed down accurately, in verse, for two thousand generations. There are stories that describe volcanic eruptions that geologists have dated to thirty- seven thousand years ago. Europe is a very young place telling itself old stories. Australia is an old place telling itself much older ones. You are flying between the two. Sleep well.

Told to the little one · The story of every story

Every fairy tale ever told

Almost every story you have told her these three weeks has the same shape. A child leaves home. Goes out into the wide world. Meets something strange — a talking wolf, a wandering troll, a swan with a name, a small man asking her to guess his. Learns something. Comes home changed. Peer Gynt. The Little Mermaid. The Ugly Duckling. Snow White. Krtek the Mole. Peter and the Wolf. It is the same story every time. It is the story of your family this month, too. When she asks tomorrow whether the holiday is over, tell her the truth. No — this is only the last chapter. The one where you come home changed. The story ends when you walk in your own front door. That is where the memory of it starts.
