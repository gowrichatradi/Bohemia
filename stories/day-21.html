Day 21 · Sun 18 Oct · Home

Stories, myths & legends

The long way south. A ghost in the airport metro tunnel. Pilots' folklore about the Milky Way route over Siberia. And, waiting for you at the other end, the oldest stories on the trip — the Dreamtime spirits of the land you're returning to.

The Ghost Line, one last time

Kastrup M2 · driverless

You started with this story. On the way out you might see it again. The M2 has no driver, so its front window looks straight into the tunnel — and drivers of passing trains have, since 2008, occasionally reported a figure standing at the front of the northbound carriage. The operations centre explains, correctly, that a lit carriage becomes a mirror at speed and any silhouette is your own reflection. The drivers still call it the Ghost Line. Ride it once more, at the front. Look for yourself.

The pilots' Milky Way

SQ351 · CPH → SIN

On the northern polar-adjacent routes — CPH to SIN, London to Tokyo — the plane crosses at 40,000 feet over Siberia, above the auroral oval and out of range of most weather. Long-haul pilots have a private name for the view on a clear polar night: the Milky Way road. The stars, undimmed by any city glow for thousands of kilometres, are said to be so dense they look like a paved surface you could walk on. On the CPH-SIN leg, if you have a window seat on the right side of the plane and the shades are up around 04:00 local time, you get it. Ask the cabin crew when the lights will be dimmed. Try to be awake for that hour. Then sleep.

Andersen would have loved the metro

Kastrup · driverless since 2007

Hans Christian Andersen wrote The Emperor's New Clothes — a story about the danger of not saying what you can obviously see. He also wrote The Story of a Mother, The Bell, and The Snow Queen, all about journeys. He would (surely) have loved the driverless carriages of the M2 — a train that runs itself with no one at the wheel, so honestly and matter-of-factly that no one bothers to notice any more. It is the anti-Emperor's-New-Clothes: a thing that works, without needing to explain itself. He would have written a story about it. You are living it instead.

The whirlwinds of home

Melbourne · Aboriginal Dreamtime

In several Aboriginal traditions across southern Australia, the small dust-devils that spin across the open ground at dusk — willy willies — are spirits, sometimes ancestors, sometimes messengers, carrying stories from one country to another. They are not to be walked through, and are best acknowledged with a quiet nod when they pass. Whether you believe or not, when you get home there is a good chance one will pass across an open paddock in the first week you're back — and after three weeks in old-Europe folklore, you might, briefly, feel like nodding at it. That is the Kulin countries greeting you home.

Told to the little one · The story of a journey

Every fairy tale ever

Almost every fairy tale you have told her on this trip has the same shape — a child leaves home, goes out into the wide world, meets strange things, learns something, and comes home changed. Peer Gynt. The Little Mermaid. The Ugly Duckling. Peter and the Wolf. All journeys, and all return. On the last flight, if she asks whether the trip is over, tell her: no, we are in the last chapter of the story — the coming home. That is when the changes settle. Sleep on it. Land tomorrow. Home is where the story ends and the memory of it starts.
