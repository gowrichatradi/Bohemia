Day 20 · Sat 17 Oct · Copenhagen — rest day

Stories, myths & legends

A pleasure garden opened to distract the citizens from a revolution. A church three generations of the same family built, and none of them lived to see finished. A pastry invented for cyclists that promised superhuman endurance. An aquarium shaped like a whirlpool because the architect dreamed of one.

Tivoli, or "when the people amuse themselves"

Tivoli Gardens · opened 1843

Tivoli was founded during a wave of unrest across Europe. Georg Carstensen, a journalist and impresario, pitched the idea directly to King Christian VIII with the line "when the people amuse themselves, they don't think of politics". The king granted the licence and the land within a month. Tivoli opened in 1843 and Copenhagen politics did in fact calm down. Walt Disney visited in 1951 and 1952, took notes, and went home to build the version without the beer garden. Ride the wooden roller coaster (1914) — it is still braked by a man riding in the middle of the train with his hand on a lever. He is not a mascot. He is a brakeman.

Grundtvig's Kirke — three generations of Klint

Grundtvigs Kirke · 1921–1940

The stepped yellow-brick facade of Grundtvig's Kirke looks like a giant church organ. The architect, Peder Vilhelm Jensen-Klint, is said to have designed the whole thing in one night in 1913 after the death of his wife. He worked on it until his own death in 1930, at which point his son Kaare Klint (already a famous furniture designer) took over and finished it in 1940. Kaare's son Esben Klint later designed the church's lights. Three generations of Klint fingerprints in one building. Copenhageners will tell you the church "belongs" to that family more than to Grundtvig, whom it commemorates.

The Sportskage of 1891

Conditori La Glace · founded 1870

La Glace's signature cake — the Sportskage ("Sports Cake") — is a mound of caramelised choux, whipped cream, and macaroons on a base of sponge. It was invented in 1891 for the opening night of a theatre play about a bicycle race. Copenhagen cyclists in the following years took it up as a race-day breakfast on the reputation that it "gave superhuman endurance", a claim taken seriously enough that the Danish cycling federation once (briefly) considered whether it constituted a performance-enhancing substance. It did not. It is delicious, and one slice will end all discussion of dinner.

The aquarium the architect dreamed

Den Blå Planet · 2013 · Kim Herforth Nielsen (3XN)

Northern Europe's largest aquarium is shaped, from above, as a five-armed whirlpool spiralling into the sea. The lead architect, Kim Herforth Nielsen, has said the form came to him "in a dream about a whirlpool" during the design phase. Whether or not that's the honest story, the building was built as an actual spiral — every corridor curves, no room is rectangular, and the visitor is drawn inward around the tanks the way water goes down a plughole. It works. The whirlpool leads to the ocean tank at the centre, with the hammerheads.

The polar bears and the mermaid

The Little Mermaid statue · 1913 · Edvard Eriksen

The Little Mermaid statue at Langelinie was commissioned in 1909 by Carl Jacobsen, the Carlsberg beer heir. He'd seen the ballet, fallen in love with the ballerina Ellen Price, and asked her to model. She refused to pose nude — so Eriksen used Ellen's face and his own wife Eline's body. The statue is 1.25m tall, smaller than everyone expects, and has been decapitated (1964), lost her arm (1998), splashed with paint (repeatedly), and knocked into the water (1998) — every time restored. Copenhagen's most photographed object is also its most bruised.

Told to the little one · The Ugly Duckling

Andersen · 1843

A duckling was born who did not look like the others. The other ducks pecked at him. The chickens laughed. He ran away. He spent a lonely winter alone on a pond. When spring came he saw beautiful white birds coming out of the reeds and, ashamed of himself, walked toward them expecting to be pecked to pieces. He looked down at the water. He was a swan. Andersen wrote it about himself — the ugly, awkward, unloved boy who reinvented himself in Copenhagen. Every child who has ever felt out of place has been told this story at least once.
