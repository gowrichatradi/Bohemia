Day 20 · Sat 17 Oct · Copenhagen — rest day

Stories, myths & legends

A pleasure garden a Danish journalist talked a king into building because "people who are amusing themselves don't think about politics" — a scheme so successful that Walt Disney visited a century later, took notes, and went home to build a copy. A writer who carried a pair of scissors in his pocket and cut paper wonders for children while he told them stories. A city with more bicycles than people, whose traffic lights are tuned to their pedals. And a cake once accused of being a performance-enhancing drug.

The park that stopped a revolution

Tivoli Gardens · opened 15 August 1843

In 1842, Europe was jittery. Riots in Paris. Revolutions expected any day. King Christian VIII of Denmark was worried the Copenhageners might get restless. A young journalist and impresario named Georg Carstensen came to the palace with a simple pitch, written on a single sheet of paper: when the people amuse themselves, they do not think of politics. Give me a piece of the old city fortifications and I will build the most beautiful pleasure garden in Europe. The king signed the licence within a month. Tivoli opened the following summer with a Chinese pagoda, an artificial lake, a merry-go-round, fireworks every night, and beer. Denmark's revolution, when it came in 1848, was among the gentlest in Europe. In 1951 Walt Disney and his wife visited Tivoli three times in a fortnight, spent an evening taking notes at a garden table, and went home. Disneyland opened four years later. It is a copy of a solution to a problem Denmark solved with kindness.

The writer with scissors in his pocket

Hans Christian Andersen · 1805–1875 · paper cuttings

Andersen carried a small pair of scissors in his pocket everywhere he went. When he told a story to a child — and he told stories to children constantly, in every country he visited — he would pull out the scissors and a folded piece of paper and, without ever looking down at his hands, cut an intricate silhouette to go with the story: a swan, a castle with turrets, a ballerina on tiptoe, a windmill, a Christmas tree hung with a hundred tiny cut apples. When the story ended, he opened the paper and gave the cutting away. He made hundreds of them. Never sold one. Never signed one. Over three hundred survive today in museums in Denmark and around the world, and each is more intricate than the last — swans hidden inside curtains, hearts folded into flowers, faces made out of birds. He is one of the greatest writers the world has ever produced, but he told children, at the end of his life, that the paper cuttings were his best work. Because he made them for you and then gave them to you.

Three generations of one family, one church

Grundtvigs Kirke · 1921–1940 · the Klints

Out at Bispebjerg, on the northern edge of the city, is a church that looks like an enormous yellow-brick church organ, six million bricks stacked into a gothic silhouette. The architect Peder Vilhelm Jensen-Klint sketched the whole building in one night in 1913, after his wife died. Building began. He worked on it for seventeen years. He died in 1930, before it was finished. His son Kaare Klint, who was already the most famous furniture designer in Denmark, put down his chairs and took over the church. Kaare finished it in 1940. Kaare's son Esben Klint later designed the church's lights. Three generations of the same family, one building. It is the only church of its size in Europe designed entirely in hand-shaped brick, and every brick was made specifically for it. Copenhageners will tell you the church belongs less to Grundtvig — a nineteenth-century pastor — than to the Klints. They are not wrong.

The cake that (nearly) got banned from cycling

Sportskagen · Conditori La Glace · 1891

La Glace has been baking cakes on the same corner in the old town since 1870. Its signature cake — a mound of caramelised choux pastry, whipped cream and macaroons on sponge — was invented for the opening night of a Copenhagen play in 1891 about a bicycle race, and named the Sportskagen, the Sports Cake. Copenhagen cyclists adopted it as a pre-race breakfast on the strength of a rumour that it "gave superhuman endurance". The rumour became so persistent that the Danish cycling federation held a meeting, in the 1890s, on whether the cake should be classified as a performance-enhancing substance. They decided, wisely, that it should not. One slice is enormous. It is not lunch. It replaces lunch, and also possibly dinner.

The aquarium built like a whirlpool

Den Blå Planet · Kastrup · 2013

Northern Europe's largest aquarium sits at the edge of the airport, and if you look at it from above it is shaped like a whirlpool. Five curved arms spiral inward to a central disk. The lead architect, Kim Herforth Nielsen of 3XN, has said the shape came to him in a dream about a whirlpool. He may or may not have meant that literally. What he certainly did was persuade his firm to build the entire aquarium as an actual spiral, so that visitors walking through it are drawn inwards, past sharks, past clownfish, past electric eels, and finally down to the great ocean tank in the centre where the hammerheads are. Nobody who has walked through it has walked out disagreeing with him. You are on your way home from Copenhagen down a plughole.

The green wave for the bicycles

Copenhagen · 62% of commutes by bicycle

Copenhagen has more bicycles than people. Six out of every ten Copenhageners ride to work every day, in every weather, in every season, in every outfit. What is beautiful is how the city treats them. On the main bike routes — Nørrebrogade, Amagerbrogade, Vesterbrogade — the traffic lights are quietly tuned so that if you cycle at exactly 20 km/h you will get every green light in sequence for four kilometres without stopping. It is called the Green Wave. There are small metal footrests at intersections so you don't have to put your foot down when you are stopped. There are cycle bridges shaped like snakes and rings and long orange ribbons flying over the harbour. There is a bicycle counter on the town hall wall that clicks up in real time as riders pass. Some days it goes past forty thousand. Nowhere else on Earth has quite understood that the fastest thing a city can do is get out of a bicycle's way.

Told to the little one · The Ugly Duckling

Andersen · 1843 · autobiographical

Once there was a little duckling on a farm who did not look like the others. He was grey and gawky and had a long silly neck. The other ducks pecked at him. The chickens laughed. The girl who fed them tried to shoo him away. He was so unhappy he ran off, and he spent a long lonely winter on his own by a frozen pond. When spring came at last he saw three beautiful white birds coming out of the reeds. He walked towards them shyly, expecting to be pecked to pieces — but they came out to meet him. And he looked down at the still water. And he was a swan. Andersen wrote that story about himself. He had been a gawky boy who was teased for his large nose and his shabby clothes, and who reinvented himself in Copenhagen. Every child who has ever felt out of place has been told his story at least once. Now she has too.
