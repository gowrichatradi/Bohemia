Day 4 · Thu 1 Oct · Reine

Stories, myths & legends

You are today at the last village on the last island at the end of the last road. In Norwegian folklore this is the sort of place selkies come to dance and blacksmiths keep an iron bird for luck. Believe or don't — everyone at the bakery does.

Å — the last letter, the last village

Å i Lofoten · population ~100

Å is the last letter of the Norwegian alphabet and this is the last village on the road. Local legend says a wandering monk named it that way on purpose — he had walked to the end of the known world and wanted the last word. There is no "next village" past Å; the road stops. Ask for a stamp at the tiny post office and they'll postmark it "Å" with a small smile — mail from the end of the alphabet.

Selkies on Skagsanden beach

Skagsanden · Flakstad

On midsummer's eve, the selkies come ashore at Skagsanden — seal-women who shed their sealskins in a neat pile above the tideline and dance on the sand until dawn. Any fisherman who finds a skin and hides it (in his rafters, wrapped in wool) marries the seal-woman it belongs to; she stays as long as the skin is hidden and vanishes back to the sea the day she finds it. The old Lofoten families claim descent from these marriages — dark-eyed, long-limbed, and always drawn back to water.

The blacksmith's iron cormorant

Sund Fiskerimuseum · working forge

The forge at Sund is still worked today. The smith there — and every smith before him — makes one small iron cormorant a year, blackened by fire, and hides it somewhere in the village: under a doorstep, on a chimney, inside a stone wall. It is bad luck to find the current year's bird before New Year's, and worse luck to move it. Ask at the forge how many are hidden in Sund today. He will tell you he doesn't know, and he is telling the truth.

The Draug and the coin on the shore

Nordic maritime folklore

The Draug is an old sailor's ghost from the Norwegian coast — wrapped in seaweed, rowing a half-boat with one oar. Not a monster. A solemn presence, a reminder that the sea has taken people. In Lofoten the custom is not to fear him but to acknowledge him: leave a coin on a flat stone above the tideline before you row. It's for him. If you find a coin someone else left, don't take it. Add one, and go on.

Sakrisøy and the yellow houses

Sakrisøy · painted ochre

Every fishing village in Lofoten is painted red, except Sakrisøy, which is painted yellow. The village will tell you the ochre was cheaper because it came from the same iron oxide as the local soil. But the older story is that a wealthy trader in the 1800s wanted his houses distinct from the fishermen, and paid three times over for yellow ochre shipped from Italy. The paint stuck. The children of that trader married fishermen. The houses are still yellow.

Told to the little one · The Huldra of the woods

Norwegian forest folklore

Huldra is a beautiful woman who lives in the forests of Norway. From the front she looks like a village girl. From the back she has a cow's tail she tries to hide. She is not wicked — she just wants company. If she offers you a cup of berries in the woods, thank her politely and go home. If you are kind to her, she rewards you with a full cow-shed for life.
