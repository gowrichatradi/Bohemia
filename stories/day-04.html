Day 4 · Thu 1 Oct · Reine

Stories, myths & legends

You have driven to the last village on the last island at the end of the last road. Past this point Norway simply runs out. So the stories get older. A dried fish that saved medieval Europe. A seal-woman married to a fisherman. And a beach in the far west that, three nights a year, is danced on by people who are not people.

The dried fish that saved Europe

Lofoten stockfish · one thousand years and counting

Look at any wooden rack in Lofoten. The cod hanging in the wind without salt or fire, twisting itself into leather over three months — this is stockfish, and it was, quietly, one of the most important foods in the history of Europe. Vikings ate it on voyages that lasted years. Catholic Europe ate it every Friday for eight hundred years, because it stays edible for a decade and reconstitutes in cold water. It funded the Hanseatic League. It crossed the Atlantic with Columbus. Portugal built an entire cuisine around it — the bacalhau in every Lisbon restaurant started on a rack you are looking at now. Lofoten did not export cod. Lofoten exported time.

The village at the end of the alphabet

Å i Lofoten · population ~100

Å is one letter. It is the last letter of the Norwegian alphabet and it means "small river". It is also the name of this village — nothing else, no suffix, no second word — because when the road runs out, the name is allowed to run out too. Locals like the joke so much that they have refused every attempt to make the name any longer. Post-office signage authorities have argued for decades. Å has won every time. Buy a postcard from the tiny post office. It will be postmarked with a single letter. There is no other address on Earth like it.

The seal-woman on the shore

Skagsanden · Flakstad · selkie folklore

The old story tells of a fisherman walking home along Skagsanden one summer's night who saw a group of women dancing on the sand. Beside them lay a pile of sealskins. He crept up, took one, and hid it in his roof-thatch. At dawn six of the women slid back into their skins and vanished into the sea. The seventh stood alone on the beach and wept. He took her home. She married him. She bore him three children. She was good to him for twelve years, and she never once looked at the sea. And then one morning she was rethatching the roof and her hand closed on something soft and silver. She held it for a long moment. She walked to the beach. She was gone before her husband got home. Every dark-haired child in a Lofoten harbour town has been told, once, that she is one of theirs.

The day the sun comes back

Solfest · the far north · every January

Because the sun disappears completely in Lofoten for two months every winter, its return is a public event. There is a known date. Everyone in the village knows it. The children make sun cakes — solboller — round yellow buns with a knob of butter and raspberry jam in the middle, glowing warmly on the plate like small suns of their own. On the morning the sun is due back, the whole village goes outside — the school class walks in a line, the fishermen stop mending nets, the shopkeeper locks the shop, the grandparents come out in coats. They stand on the beach facing south. And when the very top edge of the sun lifts above the horizon for the first time in sixty days — for maybe a minute, no more — they cheer. Then they go home and eat the buns. In Tromsø it is an official public holiday. In smaller villages it is a private one. Only a country where the sun really goes away could invent a holiday for it coming back.

The yellow houses of Sakrisøy

Sakrisøy · Reinefjord

Every fishing village in Lofoten is painted red. It is not a choice, exactly — the paint was the cheapest available on the coast, made from a rust-red iron oxide that the fishing families could afford in bulk. Only wealthy merchants and shipowners could get their hands on the imported white or yellow pigments, and so those colours became a code. A yellow house said money. Sakrisøy is entirely yellow. It was built by a single trader in the 1800s who wanted the village to be visible, in every sense, from the water. His descendants still own the buildings. They still repaint them yellow. Nobody has ever asked them to stop.

Told to the little one · The Huldra with the cow's tail

Norwegian forest folklore

Deep in the Norwegian woods there lives a lady called Huldra. From the front she looks just like anyone else — pretty, friendly, usually singing a little song. But if you can peek behind her, gently and politely, she has a long cow's tail she tries to hide inside her skirt. She is not scary. She is lonely, and she likes talking to children. If she offers you berries in the forest, say thank you and go home. If you are kind to her, she brings the cows in the village extra milk all winter.
