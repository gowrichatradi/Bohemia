Day 6 · Sat 3 Oct · Reine → Polar Park (aurora chase east)

Stories, myths & legends

The road east crosses out of Lofoten into the country of the wolves and the reindeer herders. Stories change here — the sea-eagle nests give way to bear tracks, the fishermen's ghosts to the wolf-hunters' songs, and the mountain silhouettes turn from cathedrals to something that watches you back.

The bridge trolls of Tjeldsundet

Coastal folklore · told to keep children off the bridges

The old stories say the Tjeldsund crossing was guarded by two trolls — one on each side — who let the winter storms through only when they were paid. On the night the fixed bridge opened in 1967, the sea rose to the deck by morning; the local grandmothers said the trolls had turned their backs. Every child who grew up on either side was told never to cross alone in a storm, because you could still hear the trolls arguing in the current below.

The wolves of Norway — nearly gone, brought back

Population collapse 1960s · reintroduction 1980s

By 1970 there were fewer than five wolves left in Norway — the sheep-farming lobby had hunted them almost to extinction. The current population, around 60 in the wild plus the Polar Park pack, exists because a Swedish–Norwegian legal case in the 1980s recognised them as a protected native species. Polar Park's founding wolves were rescued individuals, some from culling operations they were literally taken out of. Each has a keeper who knows its name.

The Sámi and the reindeer

Nine sub-groups · reindeer herding still legal only to Sámi

The Bardu valley you drive through today sits inside the reindeer-herding district — an area of northern Norway where, by law, only Sámi families may keep reindeer. There are nine Sámi language groups; in Bardu you are on the edge of Northern Sámi territory, and the town is officially bilingual. The stories go both ways: the older Norwegian farms tell of Sámi families who lent them food in the hard winter of 1868; the Sámi families remember the Norwegians teaching them to farm potatoes.

The lynx that came back

Extinct in Norway by 1900 · re-established 1960s

The Eurasian lynx was declared extinct in Norway around 1900 after two centuries of bounty hunting. It came back on its own, walking across the border from Sweden in the 1960s, and now there are around 400 in the wild — including the ones at Polar Park, who came from a Swedish rescue centre in the 1990s. They are the only wild cat in Scandinavia; if you see the enclosure ones, that is the animal that will never let you see it in the forest.

Told to the little one · How the wolf got its howl

A story for the drive

The old story says the wolf did not know how to howl at first. He tried to bark like a dog and it sounded silly, so he sat down at the foot of the mountain and cried. The moon was passing over that night, and she felt so sorry for him that she came down and taught him a song — the song she sings to the sky. From then on, every full moon, the wolf sings the moon's song back to her, and she comes closer to listen. That is why wolves howl at the moon. And that is why the moon looks so big on full-moon nights: she is coming down to listen.
