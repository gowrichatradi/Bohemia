Day 6 · Sat 3 Oct · Reine → Svolvær

Stories, myths & legends

The road east passes a longhouse where a Viking chieftain lived who may or may not have turned into a wolf at night, a beach where people surf inside the Arctic Circle, and a stretch of sky where you might see sea eagles with wingspans wider than you are tall. It is a lot of Norway for one afternoon.

The chieftain who turned into a wolf

Lofotr Viking Museum · Borg

Under a grassy mound at Borg archaeologists found an eighty-three metre Viking longhouse — the biggest ever discovered anywhere in the world — and inside it were drinking glasses from the Rhine, a gold ring from Byzantium, and a chieftain's mead-hall that could seat two hundred and fifty men. The chief who lived there around 900 AD was Olaf Tvennumbruni, "Olaf of the Two Eyebrows". He eventually packed everything up and sailed to Iceland — the politics got awkward — and the Icelandic sagas record his name quietly. They also record, more quietly still, that he was a hamramr: a man believed to leave his body at night, take the form of a wolf, and run the hills. Whether the neighbours meant this as a metaphor or as a warning depends on the century.

The eagles with the wingspan of a small aeroplane

White-tailed sea eagle · Lofoten · densest population in Europe

Look up over the water today and you might see them. The white-tailed sea eagle is the largest bird of prey in Europe. Fully grown, it has a wingspan of two and a half metres — three times the height of a toddler standing arms up — and it soars low over the fjords looking for fish. It is the same bird Americans call the bald eagle's larger cousin. In the middle of the twentieth century there were only twenty breeding pairs left in the whole of Norway, poisoned to the edge of extinction by farming chemicals. Now Lofoten has the densest breeding population of them anywhere on Earth. Ferry captains say you can set your watch by them — a pair off Reine will fly out over your bow at the same time every afternoon. They are so big that a first-time visitor almost always misreads them, briefly, as something engineered. Look up on the ferry. If you see an eagle, everyone else will see it too. They are hard to miss.

The eye in the rock

Uttakleiv beach · "Lofotens øye"

At one end of Uttakleiv beach is a rock formation so weirdly perfect it looks manufactured: a stone ring, roughly circular, with an oval pool of dark water inside it. From above it is unmistakably an eye. Photographers call it Lofoten's Eye. The local story is that a giant lay down here to watch the sea for enemies, fell asleep waiting, and never woke — and the rest of him became the island of Vestvågøy that you have just been driving across. On calm autumn nights the "eye" fills with rainwater and you can lie beside it and watch the aurora reflected in it, which is when you realise the giant is still watching too. That is the story, anyway. It gets told at every campfire, every summer.

Nine daughters of the sea

Unstad · Arctic surf beach

People surf here. Inside the Arctic Circle. In wetsuits so thick you can barely bend an elbow. The waves at Unstad are ruled, in the old Norse view of things, by the sea-giant Ægir and his wife Rán, and they have nine daughters — one for each kind of wave. The gentle roller is Dúfa. The one that rears up glittering is Himinglæva, the heaven-shining. The one that dumps you and holds you down is Kolga, the cold one, who is not fond of visitors. Ask any surfer here after a session and they'll tell you, without smiling, which sister they met that morning. She usually has an opinion.

Green glass on nights when the sky burns

Vikten Glasshytta · glassblowers since 1978

The Vikten glassblowers, a family workshop in a red barn on the coast, make a particular green-blue glass that you don't see anywhere else. They are usually happy to tell you it is a matter of copper oxide, furnace temperature, and a lifetime of practice. Come back the next day and buy a coffee for the older brother and he will tell you the real thing. On nights the aurora is out — the green nights, the ones where the sky moves — the glass they blow comes out brighter. Every attempt to reproduce it in the day falls slightly short. He knows how this sounds. He also has a shelf of the glass from those nights, and it doesn't quite look like the daytime shelf. See if you can pick out which is which.

Told to the little one · Thor's chariot in the sky

Norse myth · every thunderstorm

When it thunders in Norway, children are told Thor is riding his chariot across the sky. It is pulled by two very grumpy goats called Tanngrisnir (Tooth-Grinder) and Tanngnjóstr (Tooth- Gnasher). He drops his big hammer Mjölnir, and the crash is the thunder. He picks it up again — and the flash is the lightning. The best part of the story: he always picks it up. That is how Norwegian children know the storm will pass.
