Stories, myths & legends

The road east passes a Viking chieftain's hall, an eye carved in stone, a beach shaped by a sea-giant, and a glass-blower who insists the aurora colours his work. It's a lot for one drive.

Olaf of the two eyebrows

Lofotr Viking Museum · Borg

The chieftain whose 83-metre longhouse stood at Borg around 900 AD was Olaf Tvennumbruni — "Olaf of the two eyebrows". Sagas record him as a hard, capable man who left for Iceland when Norway got politically inconvenient. The Icelandic sagas also record, more quietly, that he was believed to be a hamramr — a shape-changer, who at night took the form of a wolf and ran the hills. Whether the sagas mean he behaved like a wolf, or actually became one, depends on which century you were listening in.

Lofoten's eye at Uttakleiv

Uttakleiv beach · "Lofotens øye"

On the shore at Uttakleiv is a naturally circular rock formation with a dark oval "pupil" in the centre — famous with photographers as Lofoten's Eye. Local folklore says it is the eye of a giant who fell asleep watching the sea for enemies. He never woke. The rest of him became the island of Vestvågøy. On calm nights the "eye" fills with rainwater and reflects the aurora perfectly. On stormy nights, they say, it blinks.

Ægir's nine daughters at Unstad

Unstad · Arctic surf beach

Unstad is where people surf inside the Arctic Circle. In Norse mythology the sea is ruled by Ægir, a giant, and his wife Rán. They have nine daughters, and the daughters are the waves — each one named for a kind of wave: Himinglæva (heaven-shining), Kolga (cold one), Bylgja (billow), Dúfa (dip), and so on. Surfers at Unstad who catch a good wave will still say they were "handed" it by one of the daughters. Ones that dump you: probably Kolga.

Green glass from the northern lights

Vikten Glasshytta · working glassblower

The glassblowers at Vikten produce glass in every colour, but their signature is a particular green-blue, a shade you don't see much elsewhere. Ask them where the colour comes from and they'll cite copper oxide and furnace temperature. Ask them again, over a coffee, and they'll tell you the truth: on nights when the aurora is out, the glass they blow that night comes out different — brighter green — and every attempt to reproduce it in the daytime falls slightly short. It might be nonsense. But the glass really is that colour.

Eggum and the eyeless stone

Eggum · Hoderaka sculpture (1992)

On the shore at Eggum stands a stone head — Hoderaka, by Swiss sculptor Markus Raetz — a granite face that stares out to sea with hollow-eye sockets. Placed in 1992, it looks a thousand years old. Local kids will tell you the head belongs to Egg — a giant whose body is the island — and that on the shortest days of winter, when the sun is below the horizon for weeks, the head's eyes fill with sea-water so it can still cry for the lost sun.

Told to the little one · Thor and his hammer

Norse myth · every thunderstorm

When it thunders in Norway, children are told Thor is riding his chariot across the sky, pulled by two goats named Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr ("tooth-grinder" and "tooth-gnasher"). He drops his hammer Mjölnir and the sound is thunder. He picks it up and the light is lightning. He always picks it up — that is how you know the storm will pass.
