Day 19 · Fri 16 Oct · Dresden, all day → Berlin airport

Stories, myths & legends

A church rebuilt from its own scorched rubble, every stone in its old place. Sandstone towers that are the toes of a sleeping giant. A courtyard that plays music when it rains. A tile-maker who won a wife by finishing a floor.

The Frauenkirche is its own memorial

Frauenkirche · destroyed 1945, rebuilt 1994–2005

The Frauenkirche was Dresden's baroque icon; on 13–15 February 1945 it burnt for two days and then, on the morning of the 15th, collapsed. The rubble sat as a mound in the middle of Dresden for 45 years, deliberately un-cleared, as a memorial. When it was finally rebuilt in the 1990s, every fallen stone was catalogued, scanned, and put back in its original position where possible. The result: the reconstructed church is speckled dark and pale — the dark stones are original, scorched, back in their old places. About a third of the visible fabric is the old building, held in the correct arrangement by new work. It is not restoration. It is resurrection.

The giant's toes at Bastei

Bastei · sandstone pillars · 194m

The Bastei rocks rise 194 metres over the Elbe as needle-thin sandstone towers, joined by a stone bridge built in 1851. The local children's story: a giant lay down at the Elbe to drink, fell asleep, and never got up. His body sank into the soil and only his toes still stick out — the sandstone pillars. The bridge across them is where a shoe would have been. He is not dead, just sleeping. If you jump on the bridge, they say, he twitches.

The wall that plays music when it rains

Kunsthofpassage · Hof der Elemente · 1999

In a courtyard in the Neustadt, a whole building façade is fitted with painted metal funnels, gutters and downspouts arranged as a Rube Goldberg machine. When it rains — which in Dresden it does — water runs through the pipes and the whole wall plays music: gurgles, chimes, tinny percussion. The artist, Annette Paul, said the design came from a childhood memory of standing under a leaking gutter during a thunderstorm and hearing tunes in the drips. If you visit and it isn't raining, wait ten minutes. It usually will.

Pfunds Molkerei and the tile-maker's wife

Pfunds Molkerei · 1892 · "world's most beautiful dairy shop"

Every surface in Pfunds — walls, ceiling, floor, columns — is glazed with hand-painted majolica tiles by the Villeroy & Boch workshop, listed in the Guinness Book of Records as the world's most beautiful dairy shop. The tile-maker who did the interior was said to be in love with Franziska Pfund, the owner's daughter. Her father agreed to the engagement only when the last tile was laid, on his terms. The tile-maker took two years. The last tile went up in 1892. They married the same year. This is the shop's own version. Fine to believe. Buy a wheel of cheese.

The devil built Rakotzbrücke

Rakotzbrücke · Kromlau Park · 1860

The Rakotzbrücke ("Devil's Bridge") in the Azalea and Rhododendron Park at Kromlau is a slender stone arch built to form a perfect circle with its own reflection on the water below. Local legend gives the design to the devil, who — as always — asked in exchange for the soul of the first living thing to cross. The villagers, having heard this story before, sent a goat across first. The devil, robbed, kicked one of the arch stones loose and left. The stone is (allegedly) the one at the top of the arch that looks slightly askew. Photograph the reflection. It really does form a full circle.

Semperoper and the empty balcony

Semperoper · 1841 · destroyed 1945, rebuilt 1985

Wagner premiered Rienzi, Tannhäuser and The Flying Dutchman here. Strauss premiered nine operas here. The old singers of Dresden used to say that at every premiere there is one balcony box that appears full to the performers on stage — with figures they don't recognise — and empty to the ticket-holders. The composers who premiered here, the story goes, come back. Modern performers deny this, on the record. Off the record, some ask which box it is before they debut, and refuse to look at it during the overture.

Told to the little one · Sleeping Beauty and the spinning wheel

Grimm · Dornröschen

A princess was cursed to prick her finger on a spinning wheel on her 15th birthday and sleep for a hundred years. The king ordered every spinning wheel in the kingdom destroyed. She found one anyway — old, forgotten, in a room at the top of a tower — and fell asleep, and so did the whole castle around her, and briars grew over the walls. A hundred years later a prince cut through the briars and woke her with a kiss. Modern version: hide the spinning wheels better next time. Or maybe there is no next time. She is up, and it worked out.
