Stories, myths & legends

A church that was rebuilt from its own scorched rubble — every original stone catalogued, scanned, and put back exactly where it had fallen — so that today, when you look up, you can see the dark old stones set into the pale new ones like the memory of a fire. A river fleet of paddle-steamers still working with their original 1880s coal engines. A bridge whose reflection forms a perfect circle in the water. And a wall that plays music when it rains.

The oldest working paddle-steamer fleet on Earth

Sächsische Dampfschiffahrt · steaming since 1836

Down at the Terrassenufer, on the river bank below the old town, a small navy of nine white paddle-steamers with tall red-and-black funnels moors up every evening. They are the oldest continuously operating fleet of paddle-steamers in the world. The oldest of them, the Diesbar, was built in 1884 and still runs on her original steam engine — one hundred and forty-one years old, coal-fired, gleaming brass and polished wood, tended by an engineer in blue overalls with an oily rag. Six of the boats are pre-1900. All are certified historic monuments in their own right. They take passengers up the Elbe to Meissen in exactly the way they did when Bismarck was chancellor: at eight knots, with a low chuff-chuff-chuff you can feel in your feet, past vineyards, past the sandstone cliffs, past picnic families on the bank waving. It is one of the most stubbornly delightful pieces of civil engineering in Germany. Nothing has been upgraded that didn't have to be. If you have half an hour, walk down and watch one leave.

The church that put itself back together

Frauenkirche · rebuilt 1994–2005 · from its own stones

The Frauenkirche is one of the most quietly remarkable rebuilding projects ever completed. The old baroque church had fallen down during the war and its stones sat in a great mound in the middle of the city for forty-five years. When Dresden decided in the 1990s to rebuild it, they did not build a copy. They lifted the mound apart, stone by stone, catalogued every piece, scanned it, worked out where it had come from in the original church, and put it back in the same place. Cranes moved each stone individually. Engineers used mapping software that had been written for reconstructive surgery. About 43% of the outside of the finished church is made of the original stones, each one scorched dark, sitting back in the exact position it held in 1743, keyed into pale new stone around it. The building is therefore speckled dark and pale, like a very old cake that has been rebuilt with new icing. It is not restoration. It is not a copy. It is the same church, reassembled. Look up. The blackened stones are quietly doing their old job.

The Devil's Bridge that makes a perfect circle

Rakotzbrücke · Kromlau Park · finished 1860

A little way east of Dresden, in a park full of azaleas, is a small stone bridge that arches so precisely over a still pond that when you photograph it from the right angle its reflection forms a perfect black circle in the water. It is called the Rakotzbrücke, or the Devil's Bridge, because German villagers of the nineteenth century agreed that arch this precise could only have been built by the Devil in a hurry. What in fact built it is careful masonry, patience and a beautifully surveyed curve — commissioned by a local nobleman named Friedrich Hermann Rötschke in 1860, who wanted a bridge that only looked complete when the water below it was still. He got exactly that. Nobody is allowed to walk on it any more, so the reflection is now the only way to complete the circle. Wait for a windless moment. Take the picture. It really is a circle.

The eleven sandstone pillars that are somebody's toes

Bastei · 194m above the Elbe · bridge 1851

The Bastei is a run of thin sandstone pillars rising almost two hundred metres straight out of the forest along the Elbe, connected by a slender pale stone bridge built by hand in 1851. Geology explains them as the last resistant cores of a much larger sandstone plateau that the river spent millions of years slowly filing away. Saxon parents tell their children a shorter version. A giant walked to the Elbe to drink, lay down at the bank, fell asleep, and slowly sank into the soft soil until only his toes stuck out. The bridge across them is where his shoelaces would have been. He is not dead. He is only very, very asleep. Jump on the bridge, they say, and if you're lucky you'll feel him twitch.

The wall that plays music when it rains

Kunsthofpassage · Neustadt · installed 1999

Round the back of a Dresden courtyard called the Kunsthof, a five-storey building façade has been painted turquoise and fitted with an elaborate system of metal funnels, pipes, gutters, downspouts and copper trumpets that look, from a distance, like a Rube Goldberg machine. When it rains — and it does — the water runs down the wall through all of it in sequence, and the whole building plays music. Gurgles. Clangs. High tinny chimes off the copper trumpets. Low drum-beats off the wider funnels. Every rainstorm produces a different composition. The artist, Annette Paul, said she got the idea from a childhood memory of standing under a leaking gutter during a thunderstorm and realising the drops were making a tune. If it isn't raining when you visit, wait fifteen minutes. It usually will.

The dairy shop that took two years for love

Pfunds Molkerei · 1892 · Guinness's most beautiful dairy shop

Pfunds Molkerei is a small dairy shop in Dresden's old town. Every surface — floor, walls, ceiling, columns, counter — is hand-painted majolica tile by the Villeroy & Boch workshop, all pale blues and gold and reds, angels and swans and creamers. Guinness lists it as the most beautiful dairy shop in the world. The shop's own story is that the tile-maker was in love with the owner's daughter, Franziska Pfund. Her father would not allow the engagement until the last tile was laid. The tile-maker took two years, worked six days a week, and set the final tile in the spring of 1892. He and Franziska were married the same month. There is no separate record of him. The shop is her wedding gift, if the story is true. It is still selling cheese from the same counter.

Told to the little one · Sleeping Beauty and the spinning wheel

Dornröschen · Grimm

A little princess was cursed, as a baby, to prick her finger on a spinning wheel on her fifteenth birthday and fall asleep for a hundred years. Her father the king was so alarmed that he ordered every spinning wheel in the kingdom to be brought out into a great courtyard and burned. Every one. Every last one. Fifteen years later she went exploring in a part of the castle nobody used and found a tiny old room at the top of a spiral staircase where a very old woman was, of course, spinning. She pricked her finger. She fell asleep. The whole castle fell asleep with her — the cook mid-stir, the horses mid-stamp, the flies in the air. Rose briars grew right up over the walls. A hundred years later a prince cut through them and woke her with a kiss. The kingdom yawned once, and started again.
