Day 12 · Fri 9 Oct · Aurlandsfjord → Hardanger → Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

A road over the back of a sleeping giant. A waterfall you walk behind that whispers a name. An apple orchard planted by a monk who was fleeing history. A bridge with a coin at its middle for luck.

The giant asleep beneath Aurlandsfjellet

Snow Road · 1350m plateau

The Aurlandsfjellet Snow Road climbs to a moon-like plateau — rounded, treeless, ancient. Local folklore holds that the whole plateau is the back of a giant who lay down to sleep in the last ice age and never got up. His shoulders are the two humps south of the road, his knees the peaks to the north, and his breath is the fog that comes off the mountain on autumn mornings. If you drive slowly, the villagers say, he won't wake. Truckers who take the road too fast are said to bring the mist down within an hour.

Steinsdalsfossen and the whispered name

Steinsdalsfossen · walk-behind waterfall

The falls at Steinsdalen have a walking path that goes right behind the sheet of water — a rare arrangement. The old story: walk behind the falls three times, in silence, and the water will whisper the name of your true love. It only works if you speak to no one during the loops, and if you don't already know the name. Modern tourists who try it usually laugh and break the silence within the first pass, so the falls whisper nothing. The old people of Norheimsund say this is the correct outcome: it protects the loves already found.

The Ulvik apples of the fleeing monk

Ulvik · Hardanger apple country

Hardanger grows apples improbably well for its latitude — 60°N, the same as Anchorage. The founding story: a Cistercian monk fleeing the Reformation in 1537 carried a bundle of apple grafts north from Denmark, was sheltered by a farmer in Ulvik, and planted the first grafts in return. The original tree is (of course) said to still exist somewhere in a specific orchard, still bearing fruit, and every apple tree in Hardanger is descended from cuttings of it. Farmers who make cider will point vaguely uphill when asked which tree.

The wishing coin on Hardanger Bridge

Hardanger Bridge · 1310m suspension · 2013

Norway's longest suspension bridge. Local custom: at the middle of the span, drop a single coin (not litter — a coin) into the fjord and you are promised a return to Norway. This is a very old fjord tradition transplanted onto a very new bridge — Vikings did the equivalent by throwing silver into fjords for safe voyages. Do not throw anything larger than a coin. Do not throw a coin from a moving car. And if you have small children, hand them the coin to throw; the promise transfers.

The old Måbødalen road

Måbødalen · abandoned pre-tunnel road

Before the modern tunnel through Måbødalen (opened 1986), the road climbed the gorge on 125 hairpins carved into the cliff — you can still walk it, mossed over, waterfalls dropping past. It was said to be the road where the fjord horses learned to reverse: the switchbacks were too tight for a cart-team to turn, so the horses were trained to back up the corners on command. Old Hardanger farmers claim their fjord horses still refuse to reverse anywhere else — they'll only do it on a hairpin.

Told to the little one · The fossekall

Norway's national bird · dipper

The fossekall — the white-throated dipper — is Norway's national bird. It lives around waterfalls and dives into fast-running water for insects. Norwegian children are told that a fossekall who dips its head three times is bowing to you specifically, and it means good weather. If it flies away without dipping, don't take it personally — it's shy. Every waterfall today, watch for the small brown bird with the white throat. Bow back.
