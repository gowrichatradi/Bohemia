Day 12 · Fri 9 Oct · Aurlandsfjord → Hardanger → Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

A tongue of stone sticking out over a mile of empty air. A rock wedged between two cliffs that people stand on for a photograph. A waterfall you can walk behind. And a bridge over an old road where the horses had to learn to walk backwards, because the hairpin corners were too tight for a horse to turn around in.

The Troll's Tongue

Trolltunga · Hardangervidda · 700m sheer drop

Above the fjord, in the Hardanger, a flat sliver of rock sticks straight out of the cliff face at right angles, extending seven metres over nothing. Seven hundred metres of nothing. It is called Trolltunga — the Troll's Tongue — and the story is exactly what it sounds like. A troll was arguing with the sun about who was bigger. He stuck out his tongue at her to prove his point. The sun caught him mid-insult, turned him to stone where he stood, and left his tongue behind sticking out over the mountain forever. It sits ten hours' round-trip walk from the nearest road. Hundreds of people photograph themselves standing on it every day in season. Nobody has fallen off it. Yet.

The rock that got stuck

Kjeragbolten · Lysefjord · 984m above sea

Not far south of your route today, a five-cubic-metre boulder about the size of a small van sits wedged in a crack between two cliff faces, nearly a kilometre straight up from the fjord below. It has been there for ten thousand years, held only by friction, since the last ice age dropped it in the crack and left. Every hiker who reaches it steps out onto the rock, over the abyss, and has their photograph taken. It is one of the most photographed pieces of stone in Norway. Once every century or so a geologist gets nervous and quietly measures the wedge to see if it has shifted. It hasn't. The rock does not care about anyone's opinion.

The waterfall that whispers a name

Steinsdalsfossen · walk-behind waterfall

Steinsdalsfossen is a waterfall you can walk behind. A path was built along the rock in the 1800s so that the postmen who used to run the mail through this valley could shortcut the ravine. The Hardanger story attached to it: walk behind the waterfall three full loops, in complete silence, and the water will whisper the name of the person you are meant to love. It works only if you speak to no one, and only if you don't already know the name. Tourists laugh, break the silence within the first loop, and the water whispers nothing. Old villagers in Norheimsund say this is exactly right. It protects the loves already found.

The apples of the fleeing monk

Ulvik · Hardanger apple country · 60°N

The Hardanger orchards produce apples improbably well for their latitude — sixty degrees north, the same as Anchorage. The reason is a warm south-facing microclimate off the fjord, but the story people prefer is the one about the monk. In 1537, when the Reformation reached Denmark, a Cistercian monk from the great apple monastery at Sorø slipped out of the country with a bundle of grafts wrapped in wet cloth under his cloak. He walked north for weeks, taking shelter with farmers. A widow in Ulvik gave him a barn to sleep in. In the morning he cut his grafts onto her wild trees and disappeared north. Those trees became this valley. When a farmer in Ulvik hands you a slice of apple, you are eating a piece of the last thing the monk did before he walked off the map.

The road where the horses walked backwards

Måbødalen · pre-1986 road · 125 hairpins

The old road up the Måbødalen gorge climbed 125 hairpin bends carved into the cliff wall. It was the only way east until they drove a modern tunnel through the mountain in 1986. The hairpins were so tight that a horse and cart could not turn around in one. So the horses were trained to reverse — to walk backwards around each bend, on command, all the way up. It took a specific breed to do this, the small stubby Norwegian fjord horse, which is patient and has good spatial sense. Old Hardanger farmers still swear that a proper fjord horse will only reverse on a hairpin. Ask one to back up in a field and it looks at you as if to say: where's the bend?

Told to the little one · The little bird that bows

Fossekall · Norway's national bird · the dipper

The fossekall — the white-throated dipper — is a small brown bird about the size of a fist with a bright white bib, and it is Norway's national bird. It lives right next to waterfalls, and it walks — actually walks — along the bottom of fast rivers looking for insects, holding its wings tight and gripping the stones with its little feet. Norwegian children are told that a fossekall who dips its head three times is bowing to you specifically, and today will be a good day. Bow back to it. It is polite.
