Day 2 · Tue 29 Sep · Copenhagen

Stories, myths & legends

A staircase that climbs the wrong way. A country founded by a flag that fell out of the sky. A neighbourhood that seceded from Denmark in 1971 and is still, technically, its own place. And an amusement park in a beech wood that has been open, without a break, since 1583 — the oldest in the world.

The flag that fell out of the sky

Dannebrog · 15 June 1219 · Tallinn

The Danish flag is the oldest continuously used flag in the world. It has been red with a white cross since at least 1219, and the story of where it came from is one Danish children know before they can read. King Valdemar was losing a battle in Estonia. The Danes were about to break. Then, according to the chronicles, a red cloth with a white cross drifted down from the clouds and settled at his feet. A voice was heard. The Danes rallied and won the field. The date — 15 June — is still Denmark's flag day. The name of the flag, Dannebrog, means "the cloth of the Danes". Nobody has ever been able to explain what fell.

The staircase that goes the wrong way

Vor Frelsers Kirke · Christianshavn · 1752

Look up at the copper spiral wound around the outside of the church spire. It turns counter-clockwise as it climbs — the wrong way, in every other spire in Europe. Every Copenhagen schoolchild is told the same story: on the day the spire was unveiled, the architect Lauritz de Thurah realised he had built it back to front, walked to the top, and threw himself off. He did not. He died in bed twenty- seven years later, comfortably, of natural causes. But the legend is told to schoolchildren so relentlessly that half the city still believes it, and the staircase now feels tragic even though it isn't. You can climb it. There is nobody at the bottom to catch you if history was right.

The country inside the country

Freetown Christiania · founded 26 September 1971

One September morning in 1971, a group of squatters walked through the fence of an abandoned military base on the edge of central Copenhagen and simply refused to leave. They put up a hand-painted sign — "The Free Town of Christiania" — declared themselves independent, wrote their own laws, and started running the place as a car-free commune of about a thousand people. That was over fifty years ago. They are still there. Denmark has occasionally tried to negotiate them out, occasionally sent police in, and eventually made a treaty. There is a stone at the exit that reads "You are now entering the EU". Walk in the other direction and you leave it.

The oldest working amusement ride in the world

Bakken · Klampenborg · opened 1583

A short train ride north of central Copenhagen, in a beech wood outside the palace of Klampenborg, is a small amusement park called Dyrehavsbakken — Bakken for short. It has been open for four hundred and forty-two years. Since 1583. That makes it the oldest still-operating amusement park in the world, older by two hundred and sixty years than Tivoli, older by three hundred and seventy years than Disneyland. It began as a natural spring that Copenhageners walked out to for the water, where entertainers started setting up stalls to earn a coin from the visitors. Then rides. Then wooden rollercoasters. The current wooden rollercoaster at Bakken, called Rutschebanen, has been rattling since 1932 and is still braked by hand — a real person rides in a small cabin at the back of every train with a wooden lever, watching the track and slowing the cars for the sharpest corners. He is not a mascot. He is the brake. It is one of the last of its kind on Earth.

Isak the bear who knew when storms were coming

Copenhagen Zoo · 1938–1959

Isak arrived from Greenland in 1938, the zoo's first male polar bear. His keeper — a man who kept detailed diaries because he had nothing else to do during the war — noticed that two or three days before a really bad storm, Isak would stop eating fish and start pacing a tight figure-of-eight in his enclosure. The pattern held often enough that during the German occupation the zoo director took to ringing the naval weather bureau whenever Isak paced. The bureau began to check its own charts. On one occasion in the winter of 1943, the storm arrived when the barometer said it shouldn't. A small brass plaque near the Arctic Ring today reads only Isak — the bear who knew.

Told to the little one · The little fir tree

Andersen · 1844

Once there was a little fir tree in a Danish forest who wanted to grow up. He was the smallest tree in the wood and he was always in a hurry. He wished he were taller. He wished he were older. He wished it were tomorrow, and the day after that, and the day after that. One day some men came with an axe and took him away, and put him in a warm room with candles and gold apples and glittering things, and for one evening he was the most beautiful tree in the world. It went by so fast he barely noticed it was happening. The moral, which Andersen never says out loud: notice the day you're in. Even a little fir tree gets one.
