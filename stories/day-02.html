Day 2 · Tue 29 Sep · Copenhagen

Stories, myths & legends

A tsar rode a horse to the top of a tower. An architect built a staircase the wrong way round. A polar bear predicted the weather. Copenhagen keeps its receipts.

The tsar rode a horse up the Round Tower

Rundetårn · 1716

Christian V built Rundetårn with a wide spiral ramp instead of stairs so instruments could be hauled to the observatory. In 1716 Tsar Peter the Great — 2.03m tall and famously a show-off — rode his horse to the top and back down. His wife Catherine, unwilling to be outshone, followed in a six-horse carriage that (barely) fit the ramp. There is a plaque at the entrance recording this because otherwise no one would believe it.

The spire that spirals the wrong way

Vor Frelsers Kirke · Christianshavn · 1752

Look up at the copper spiral staircase wound around the outside of the church tower. It turns counter-clockwise as it climbs — the "wrong" way. Legend has it that the architect, Lauritz de Thurah, realised his mistake on the day of the unveiling and threw himself from the top rather than face the king. He didn't. He died in bed twenty-seven years later, comfortably, of natural causes. But the story is told to every schoolchild in Copenhagen, and half of them believe it.

Isak the weather-bear

Copenhagen Zoo · 1938–1959

Isak was the zoo's first male polar bear, arrived from Greenland in 1938. Keepers noticed that in the two or three days before a severe winter storm he would pace figure-eights in his enclosure and refuse fish. The pattern held often enough that during the war the zoo director rang the weather bureau when Isak paced. There is no scientific record of this. There is a small brass plaque near the Arctic Ring today that says only Isak — the bear who knew.

The pearls no queen will wear

Rosenborg Treasury

In the treasury under Rosenborg is a rope of black pearls said to have belonged to Queen Sophie Amalie (d. 1685), a woman disliked at court. Three queens who tried them on afterwards died within a year, and by 1720 the pearls were locked in a drawer marked only "Do not wear." They are on display today. No royal woman has put them on in three hundred years.

Andersen and the tinderbox in the canal

Nyhavn 20 · 1834

Andersen wrote The Tinderbox in his first week at Nyhavn 20 — a poor soldier's story with three dogs whose eyes are the size of teacups, mill-wheels, and the Round Tower itself. Copenhageners will still tell you a real tinderbox was fished from the canal outside No. 20 the year the tale was published. Nothing in any museum backs this up. Every guide on every canal boat repeats it anyway.

Told to the little one · The Fir Tree

Andersen · 1844

A little fir tree in the forest wanted to be older, taller, and grander. One day men came and cut him down, and he was dressed in tinsel and gold apples and stood in a warm room for one glittering evening. It was everything he'd wished for. He didn't notice how brief it was until it was over. Andersen's gentlest warning: pay attention to good days while they are happening.
