Day 10 · Wed 7 Oct · Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

Bergen is famously rainy. A composer took musical dictation from trolls in the rocks below his house. The stave church is guarded by dragons. And somewhere among the wooden Hanseatic wharves is a cat who has been on duty for six hundred years.

Grieg took dictation from trolls

Troldhaugen · "Troll Hill" · Grieg's cottage

Edvard Grieg named his house Troldhaugen — Troll Hill — and meant it. He composed in a small wooden hut at the bottom of the garden, ten metres from a rock outcrop that (he insisted) housed a family of trolls. He would sometimes hear knocking on the outside wall of the hut and stop to transcribe the rhythm; several manuscripts have three short beats pencilled in the margin, exactly the pattern he described. "In the Hall of the Mountain King", from Peer Gynt, he said came to him in a dream after one of these episodes. Even people who don't believe in trolls tend, at Troldhaugen, to lower their voice near the rock.

The Bryggen cat

Bryggen wharves · Hanseatic quarter

The Hanseatic merchants at Bryggen kept cats to control the rats around the stockfish stores — this is documented. The story that isn't documented, but every guide will tell you, is that one particular cat kept coming back: a large grey with a white bib, seen guarding the wharves in every century since the 1400s, from the 1702 fire, through the 1944 harbour explosion, to now. Sightings still happen. Photographs of the current Bryggen cats — there are always a few — inevitably include one that "looks about right".

Fantoft's dragons

Fantoft Stave Church · reconstructed 1997

The dragon heads carved on stave church ridges are the church's guardians against the old pagan spirits of the forest — Christian architecture that quietly hedges its bets. Fantoft was built in Fortun in 1150, moved to Bergen in 1883, burnt down in 1992, and rebuilt exactly in 1997. The rebuilders are said to have chosen every plank by tapping it and listening for the note; timber that sang got in, timber that didn't was rejected. Whether the dragons had to be re-consecrated is a matter of opinion — the priest at the reopening did it, on the reasoning that it costs nothing.

Why Bergen rains

Bergen · ~230 rainy days a year

The meteorological answer is that Bergen sits at the front of the Atlantic weather sweeping over the coast and getting squeezed against the mountains. The Bergen answer is that the seven mountains around the city are seven old trolls who were caught by the sunrise and turned to stone, and every rainy day is their families weeping for them. Bergensere are proud of the rain — the local saying is "we don't have bad weather, we have bad clothing". Buy an umbrella at the market and consider yourself insured against both meteorology and folklore.

The fisketorget's talking cod

Bergen fish market

A market story: the fishermen of Bergen used to say that on the last day of Lent, the biggest cod on the stall would speak once, in a low voice, and tell the fishmonger who would buy him. The correct response was to raise your price for that customer by exactly one øre, no more, no less. Too little and the cod cursed the season's catch. Too much and the buyer never returned. This is patent nonsense. Old market families in Bergen will still argue that they haven't tested it, on the entirely reasonable grounds that if it's true it should not be tested.

Told to the little one · Peer Gynt and the Mountain King

Ibsen / Grieg

Peer Gynt was a Norwegian boy who told such wonderful lies that he began to believe them. In one of his adventures he wandered into the Hall of the Mountain King, deep inside a mountain, where the trolls tried to make him one of them by fitting him with a tail. He escaped by being cleverer than the trolls. Grieg's music for this part is the one that goes tum-tum-tum-tum, tum-tum-tum-tum, tum-tum-tum-tum-TUMMM. Hum it to her at Troldhaugen. She'll know.
