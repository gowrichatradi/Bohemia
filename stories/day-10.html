Day 10 · Wed 7 Oct · Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

Wooden wharves that lean into each other because they have been quietly sinking for four hundred years into the fish waste underneath. Wooden churches held together with pegs and no iron nails, still standing at eight hundred years old. A composer who spent his mornings taking dictation from trolls knocking on his little garden hut. And a whole box of medieval text messages found under a wharf, one of them signed by the man who called himself the ugliest in Bergen.

The medieval texts nobody was meant to read

Bryggen · rune sticks · rediscovered 1955

When a fire went through the Bryggen wharves in 1955, restorers digging through the ash pulled up over six hundred wooden sticks the size of pencils, carved with runes. Nobody had known they existed. They turned out to be everyday medieval Norwegian messages — the equivalent of text messages — sealed in the earth under the wooden floorboards for eight hundred years. Business letters. Trade orders. And, memorably, a small stick from around the year 1200 carved with the words: Gyda tells you to come home. There is another that just says: Kiss me. One is a schoolboy's spelling exercise. And one, in careful runes, reads: The one who wrote these runes is the ugliest in Bergen. He signed it. His name has come down to us. He is the world's oldest still-remembered joker.

Grieg's trolls in the rocks

Troldhaugen · "Troll Hill" · Grieg's cottage

Edvard Grieg named his house Troldhaugen — Troll Hill — and he did not mean it as decoration. He composed in a small wooden hut at the bottom of the garden, ten metres from a mossy rock which he insisted housed a family of trolls. He said knocks came from the outside of the hut. He wrote the rhythms down. In the corner of at least one manuscript there are three short pencilled beats in the margin — exactly the pattern he described the trolls making. "In the Hall of the Mountain King", the piece everyone knows, he said came to him after one of these visits. Grieg was only 152 centimetres tall — barely 5 foot — and got mistaken for a boy for most of his life. His piano bench had a specially raised cushion so he could reach the pedals. It is still there. You can sit at it.

The wooden churches built without a single nail

Fantoft & the Norwegian stave churches

Nine hundred years ago, Norway was covered in wooden churches with dragon heads carved along the roof ridges — several thousand of them, spread across every fjord and valley in the country. Their builders were shipwrights, so they built the churches the way they built Viking longships: no iron nails, no glue, no mortar. Every joint is a slotted wooden peg hand-fitted to the beam next to it, the whole thing keyed together by geometry. When the Norwegians converted to Christianity, they were nervous about upsetting the old forest spirits, so they carved snarling dragon heads on the church ridges as a hedge, just in case. Twenty-eight of these churches still stand in Norway, some over eight hundred years old. That is every stave church remaining on Earth. The one at Fantoft is the closest to Bergen; the timbers still smell of pine tar because the shipwrights waterproof them the way they would a boat. Push on the wall gently. The whole church rocks slightly, on purpose, like a boat at anchor. That is the reason they are still standing.

The seven mountains that were once trolls

Bergen · rain 231 days a year

The meteorological reason it rains in Bergen 231 days a year on average is that the city sits in a bowl of seven mountains that catch every drop of moisture off the North Atlantic. The Bergensere prefer a different explanation. The seven mountains were once seven old trolls who were out too late one night, caught by the rising sun on the coast, and turned to stone where they stood. Every rainy day is their wives and their children, still weeping for them from inside the rock. The local saying — repeated so often you will hear it three times today — is vi har ikke dårlig vær, vi har dårlige klær: we do not have bad weather, we have bad clothes. Buy an umbrella at the market. Consider yourself insured against both meteorology and folklore.

The wharves that hold each other up

Bryggen · sixty-two wooden buildings · UNESCO

The row of leaning pointed wooden buildings along Bergen harbour is one of the strangest sights in Europe. There are sixty-two of them, tall and thin and crooked, all painted different colours, and no two are parallel. Some of them lean left. Some lean right. Some lean forwards or backwards. The reason is that they have been sinking, slowly and unevenly, into three hundred years of fish waste. In the medieval Hanseatic period, the wharves were built on top of layers of discarded cod bones and rope and wood shavings, and the layers keep slowly settling under the weight of the buildings. So the buildings, all of them, lean into their next-door neighbours, and are held up almost entirely by leaning against each other. If you removed one, the two on either side would topple. The engineers who work here maintain them not as buildings but as a single wooden system, like a collapsed accordion still making music. Walk down the alley behind them. Notice how the whole street tilts.

Told to the little one · Peer Gynt in the Mountain King's hall

Ibsen / Grieg

Peer Gynt was a Norwegian boy who told such enormous lies that he began to believe them himself. In one of his stories he wandered into a hall deep inside a mountain, where the Mountain King and all the trolls lived, and they nearly kept him — they even fitted him with a nice green tail. He escaped by being cleverer than the whole cave. Grieg wrote the music for that scene, and it goes tum-tum-tum-tum, tum-tum-tum-tum, tum-tum-tum-tum- TUMMM. Hum it to her at Troldhaugen. She'll know.
