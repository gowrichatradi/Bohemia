Day 18 · Thu 15 Oct · Prague morning → Dresden

Stories, myths & legends

An alchemist locked in a castle by a king and told to make gold — who failed, and, over five years and twenty-four thousand experiments, accidentally invented Europe's first porcelain instead. A green diamond of a colour nature made in the dark. A king strong enough to bend horseshoes with his hands. And a whole porcelain wall of thirty-five galloping princes that has stood on the same street for more than a hundred years, made of tiles that will simply not burn.

The prisoner who invented porcelain

Johann Friedrich Böttger · Meissen · 1701–1710

Johann Friedrich Böttger was nineteen years old when he claimed in a Berlin tavern that he could turn base metal into gold. The Prussian king heard about it. Böttger, alarmed, slipped over the border into Saxony, where Augustus the Strong caught up with him and, in the friendliest possible manner, informed him that he was now the royal alchemist. He was moved into a comfortable laboratory in the fortress of Königstein and asked, with increasing urgency over the years, to please produce some gold. He could not make gold. Two things saved him. One was a much more famous scientist, Ehrenfried von Tschirnhaus, who was already trying to work out the Chinese secret of hard-paste porcelain and needed an assistant. The other was the fact that Böttger was, actually, brilliant at chemistry. Over five years, in the dungeon- laboratory under the castle, working from a small furnace and a growing library, Böttger and Tschirnhaus ran twenty-four thousand recorded experiments on clay, feldspar, and heat. In January 1708 they cracked it. Europe's first true porcelain came out of that furnace. It made Meissen's fortune. It made Saxony's fortune. Meissen porcelain is still made today, three hundred years later, on the same site, using a formula whose direct ancestor came out of the notebook of a very frightened teenager who had told a lie in a Berlin bar. Every teacup, every figurine, every plate traces back to him. The recipe he thought would kill him made a whole city.

The porcelain wall that didn't burn

Fürstenzug · 23,000 tiles · installed 1907

A wall in the old town of Dresden shows a procession of thirty-five margraves, electors and kings on horseback — the entire ruling house of Saxony from 1127 to 1904, laid out at life size across 102 metres of city wall. It is made of 23,000 individual hand-painted Meissen porcelain tiles, fired at 1400 degrees. Because they were fired at such an extreme temperature to begin with, the tiles simply cannot burn. When Dresden went through a great fire at the end of the war, the buildings all around this wall came down. The wall did not. When Dresden was rebuilt, the tiles were still in place, in perfect order, princes untouched. Every single tile you see today is the exact tile that was set into that wall in 1907. Not one has been replaced. It is one of the most stubborn pieces of art in Europe.

The green diamond that made itself in the dark

Dresden Green · 41 carats · Grünes Gewölbe

The Dresden Green is the largest naturally green diamond ever found. It was mined in Golconda in India some time before 1725, and it weighs 41 carats — about the size of a small acorn. Nothing about the colour is decorative. It is not dye. It is not tinted glass. The green comes from millions of years of exposure to natural radiation deep in the earth, which knocked microscopic imperfections into the diamond's crystal lattice and shifted the way it absorbs light. Under a reading lamp it still faintly glows. It is one of the few objects on Earth whose colour is a record of radioactive geology from a billion years ago. Augustus III of Saxony bought it in 1741 for the price of five palaces and had it set into a hat pin. It has been hidden in a fortress vault, moved to Moscow after the war, returned in 1958, and displayed in the Green Vault ever since. It has not left Saxony in nearly three centuries. Look at it long enough and you can see the darkness that made it.

The king who bent horseshoes with his hands

Augustus the Strong · Elector of Saxony · d. 1733

Augustus the Strong ruled Saxony from 1697 to 1733. His nickname was earned, not given. In front of visiting ambassadors he would casually pick up a horseshoe, put a gentle bend in it with his bare hands, and use it to prop open a door. Extending two fingers, he could hold up a fully armoured knight. He was said, in the polite tradition, to have fathered 365 illegitimate children — one for every day of the year. The truth is more like ten or twelve acknowledged, but he did not deny the larger figure and often encouraged it. He converted from Lutheran to Catholic overnight to become King of Poland — a cynical move so brazen that Voltaire admired it. He collected porcelain the way other kings collected mistresses, and had a special disease named for it: porcelain sickness. When he died in 1733 his heart was buried separately in Poland at his own request, so that a piece of him would always be Polish.

The nine-minute heist

Grünes Gewölbe · 4:57 a.m. · 25 November 2019

Just before five in the morning on the last Monday of November 2019, somebody set fire to a distribution box near the castle to cut power to the museum's alarms and streetlights. A window on the west side of the Green Vault was already weakened; the bars had been quietly sawn through some weeks earlier. Four men climbed in, ran to a single vitrine, smashed it with an axe, filled a bag with twenty-one pieces of eighteenth-century diamond jewellery — worth 113 million euros — and were out in under nine minutes. It was one of the largest thefts in modern European history. Three years later, most of the pieces were recovered in a shopping bag traded in exchange for reduced sentences. Some were bent. Some had stones missing. The Dresden Green was in another room, which is the only reason it is still on display. The axe marks are still visible in some of the recovered settings. Not folklore. This is fresh gossip.

The mosque that isn't a mosque

Yenidze · cigarette factory · 1909

There is a mosque on the Dresden skyline, complete with a coloured glass dome and two tall minarets, and it has never been a mosque for a day of its existence. In 1907 a Dresden cigarette maker called Hugo Zietz wanted to build a factory in the historic centre and market himself as a maker of exotic oriental tobacco. City regulations forbade any industrial chimney downtown. His solution: build the whole factory in the shape of an Ottoman mosque, with the two chimneys disguised as minarets. He named it the Yenidze, after the Turkish town where his tobacco came from. The Dresden clergy protested. The city noted that the minarets were, in the strictest legal sense, minarets — decorative elements — and permitted the building. It became one of the most famous industrial buildings in Germany. It is now offices. The glass dome is lit from inside every night. It is not, and has never been, a mosque. It just decided to look like one.

Told to the little one · The little man who could not be named

Rumpelstiltskin · Grimm · Saxon folklore

A miller was so proud of his daughter that he told the king she could spin ordinary straw into gold. The king, who liked the sound of that, locked her in a room full of straw and told her to prove it or die. She sat down and cried. Suddenly a tiny strange man appeared in the room and said, if I spin your straw into gold, will you give me your first child? She agreed. He spun. She was saved. Years later she was a queen and had a baby, and the little man came back. You must guess my name, he said, or I take him. She sent servants out to search. And late one night, deep in the woods, one of the servants heard the little man dancing around his fire singing his own name to himself. In the morning she said it. Rumpelstiltskin! He was so furious he stamped his foot straight through the floorboards. Never leave your name lying around.
