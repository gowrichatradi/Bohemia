Day 8 · Mon 5 Oct · Svolvær → Oslo

Stories, myths & legends

A ship built to be squeezed by the pack ice instead of crushed by it — and to drift, sealed shut, across the whole Arctic Ocean. A hill above Oslo that was called "the scream" long before anyone drew it. And an explorer who decided, at fifteen, that he was going to be the first man to the South Pole, and slept every winter with his window open for twenty years to get ready.

The ship built to be frozen

The Fram · Fram Museum · Bygdøy

The strangest thing about the polar ship Fram — which means "forward" — is what she was built to do. She was built to get stuck. Fridtjof Nansen's theory in the 1890s was that Arctic ice drifted west to east on a slow, unseen current, so the way to reach the North Pole wasn't to break through the ice at all. You sealed a ship into it and rode with it, wherever it went, for years. His shipbuilders gave her a rounded hull that would ride up when the ice squeezed her, popping to the surface instead of splintering underneath. She was frozen in for three winters. She drifted 1,600 kilometres. Nansen and one companion left her on skis to try for the Pole, missed it, and turned back. They spent nine months in a hut of walrus hide and stones, alone, eating polar bear. They walked out. The Fram is still standing on her keel in Bygdøy today — nothing has been repaired, nothing has been changed. Stand next to the timbers. Some visitors say they hear her creak.

The boy who slept with the window open

Roald Amundsen · 1872–1928

Amundsen decided at fifteen that he was going to be the first man to reach a Pole. His mother wanted him to be a doctor. He went along with medical school for four years, and the day she died he quit. From boyhood he had slept in Oslo winters with his bedroom window wide open, no matter how cold — to teach his lungs and skin the polar nights. He learned to sew and hunt from the Inuit. He borrowed Nansen's Fram, sailed south, beat Scott to the South Pole by thirty-four days in December 1911, planted the Norwegian flag, and left a small tent behind with a letter inside for Scott to find. In 1928 he heard a rescue call from a friend's crashed airship in the Arctic. He took off in a plane to look for them and disappeared over the Barents Sea. No wreckage. No body. A single fuel tank washed up months later. He was fifty-five.

The volcano that painted The Scream

Ekeberg · Munch · sunset · January 1892

Edvard Munch was walking with two friends along the ridge at Ekeberg one evening in January 1892 when, as he wrote in his diary, "the sky suddenly became blood red". He felt as if the whole of nature were vibrating. He stopped. His friends walked on. He stood alone against the railing looking out at the fjord and painted, in his mind, the image that became The Scream. What Munch did not know is that he was looking at weather from the other side of the world. Nine years earlier, in 1883, the volcano Krakatoa had erupted in Indonesia with a force so tremendous that it threw dust and sulphur into the upper atmosphere and around the whole planet. Sunsets across Europe turned an impossible red for years afterwards. Astronomers took notes. Painters painted them. Munch caught one of the last of those Krakatoan skies over Oslo, thought the sky was screaming at him personally, and painted it. The most famous painting of anxiety of the twentieth century is also a piece of accidental meteorological evidence for a volcano that had erupted before he was old enough to walk.

The opera house that walks into the sea

Oslo Opera · 2008 · Snøhetta

You can walk up the roof of the Oslo Opera House like a beach. Its Italian marble surface tilts gently down into the harbour, and there is no railing — you simply walk up. The architects Snøhetta describe it as "a building that wants to be a ship", and there is a running joke among the city's engineers that the whole building is slowly creeping seawards on its foundations, about a millimetre a year. Extrapolate the movement, they say, and by the year 2500 it will have slid gently into the fjord. The architects, asked whether this is true or a joke, have refused to comment either way. It is either a superb piece of poetry or the world's most graceful engineering flaw.

The angry old man in Vigeland Park

Sinnataggen · Vigeland Park · installed 1940

Vigeland Park is the life's work of a single obsessive artist, Gustav Vigeland, who spent forty years carving 212 sculptures of naked human beings at every stage of life — babies, lovers, old couples, families — and arranging them along one avenue in Oslo. The most famous of them is Sinnataggen, the "Angry Little Boy", a small furious bronze toddler stamping his foot. He has been stolen twice — 1988 and 2003 — and returned both times, once with a note apologising. His left hand is polished mirror-bright by thousands of children rubbing it for luck. She is welcome to add a thumbprint of her own.

Told to the little one · The three billy goats and the troll

Asbjørnsen & Moe · 1841

Three billy goats named Gruff wanted to cross a bridge to reach a field of sweet green grass on the other side. Under the bridge lived a troll, who wanted to eat them. Little Billy Goat crossed first, and when the troll leapt out, Little said wait — my bigger brother is coming, he's much tastier. Middle Billy Goat crossed next and said the same. And Big Billy Goat came last, and when the troll jumped out at him, Big just kicked the troll off the bridge and into the river. The moral of the story, according to Norwegian parents: sometimes being small is being clever, and sometimes being big is enough.
