Day 8 · Mon 5 Oct · Svolvær → Oslo

Stories, myths & legends

Oslo is a modern city built on very old dirt. The Opera House is walking to the sea. A ship in a museum still hums to itself at night. And a hill above the city was screaming long before Munch drew it.

The Fram is still humming

Fram Museum · Bygdøy

The polar ship Fram ("forward") was deliberately built to be squeezed by pack ice rather than crushed by it — a rounded hull that rides up when the ice presses. Nansen's men, drifting with the ice for three winters in the 1890s, wrote of a low singing sound at night as the timbers took the load: "as though the ship were a woman softly complaining, though not unhappily". Standing in the darkened hall today, some visitors still say they hear a low creak from the hull — which the museum insists is just tourists on the deck above.

Amundsen and the open bedroom window

Roald Amundsen · 1872–1928

Amundsen decided as a teenager to reach the poles, and set about hardening his body for it. Family lore says he slept every winter with his bedroom window wide open — no matter how cold — to train his lungs and skin for polar nights. His mother, a widow who wanted him to be a doctor, closed the window every night. He opened it again. She gave up. He got to the South Pole in 1911, thirty-four days before Scott, wearing furs the Inuit had taught him to sew.

The Opera House walking to the sea

Oslo Opera · 2008 · Snøhetta

The Opera House is the only major building in Europe you can walk up the roof of. Its Italian marble surface tilts gently down into Bjørvika harbour. Oslo architects joke that the roof is "growing" — that the building shifts imperceptibly toward the fjord each year and, if you calculate it, will reach the water in about the year 2500. The Snøhetta partners are on record saying they hope so. "It is a building that wants to be a ship."

The hill that screamed before Munch drew it

Ekeberg · Munch's landscape

Edvard Munch was walking on Ekeberg — the hill on the east side of Oslo fjord — one evening in 1892 when, as he wrote, "the sky suddenly became blood-red" and he "sensed an infinite scream passing through nature." That vision became The Scream. What Munch didn't know: Ekeberg had been called Skriket ("the scream") for centuries by locals, because in a certain wind the trees on the hill made a keening sound. He was hearing what everyone had always heard, and drawing it for the first time.

The trolls under Aker Brygge

Oslo harbour · folklore

The Oslo city motto is Unanimiter et constanter, but the older motto, they'll tell you at Aker Brygge, is "there is a troll under every stone in Oslo". The city is built on hard granite and every foundation has to be blasted. Norwegian folklore says trolls sleep inside stone, and every new building displaces one. This is used as a joke — but the older builders still tap the first stone of a foundation with a hammer and say unnskyld ("excuse me"). It is what their fathers did.

Told to the little one · The three billy goats gruff

Asbjørnsen & Moe · 1841

Three billy goats named Gruff want to cross a bridge to eat the sweet grass on the other side. Under the bridge lives a troll. Little Billy Goat crosses first and says "wait, my bigger brother is coming, he's tastier". Middle Billy Goat crosses next and says the same. Big Billy Goat crosses last — and kicks the troll into the river. Norwegian children learn from this that being small can be cleverer than being brave. Also: never trust the biggest goat.
