Day 8 · Mon 5 Oct · Bardu / Abisko → Oslo

Stories, myths & legends

You leave the aurora country by road, drop through Narvik and out on the DY365, and land in Oslo just as the streetlights are coming on. Two stories on either end — the sunken destroyers under the E10 as you drive out, and the folk hero every Norwegian child knows waiting at the other end.

The Narvik iron-ore war

Battles of Narvik · April–June 1940 · 8,000 dead

Narvik was fought over so hard because it was the only ice-free port that could load Swedish iron ore for the German war economy. In April 1940 the town changed hands four times in eight weeks; both sides suffered catastrophically. The Allied naval force sank ten German destroyers here in two battles — half the Kriegsmarine destroyer fleet. Every year on 28 May, Narvik marks the day the Allies briefly retook the town before the general withdrawal from Norway.

Askeladden — the Ash-lad

Norwegian folk hero · in every collection since 1841

Every Norwegian child grows up with Askeladden (Ash-lad) — the youngest, dirtiest, most-underestimated brother in every folktale, who wins by picking up small useless things nobody else bothers with. In one story he collects a dead magpie, an axle-key and half a nettle; in the next he has out-tricked a troll, married the princess, and inherited the kingdom, all because those three objects turned out to matter at exactly the right moment. He is the Norwegian answer to "the tortoise and the hare" — the lesson being not slow-and-steady, but pay-attention-to-small-things.

The Kraken and Erik Pontoppidan

First serious scientific description · 1752

The Kraken — Norwegian sea-monster the size of an island — was first written up as a real biological creature by Bishop Erik Pontoppidan of Bergen in 1752. He collected fishermen's reports, measured the size, gave it a Latin name, and put it in his Natural History of Norway. Modern scholars think he was describing a colossal squid at the surface. The word Kraken has left Norwegian and gone everywhere — the beer, the rum, the film — but it started as a serious species entry in a scientific catalogue.

The scream in the sky

Munch · 22 January 1892 · Ekebergveien, Oslo

Edvard Munch was walking with two friends along Ekebergveien above Oslo on the evening of 22 January 1892 when the sunset went red — probably from Krakatoa ash still in the atmosphere nine years after the eruption. His diary entry: "I stood there trembling with anxiety — and I sensed an endless scream passing through nature." The Scream came out of that walk, painted the following year. The road is still there; the view is now blocked by trees, but you can walk it. It's ten minutes from Oslo Opera by tram 18.

Vigeland's Angry Boy

Sinnataggen · cast 1928 · missing 1990–1991

The most-photographed bronze in Norway is a very small child mid-tantrum, one clenched fist raised, foot stamped on the granite of the Vigelandspark bridge. Everyone rubs his left hand for luck, which is why it is smooth and gold. In 1990 he was stolen — dug up in the middle of the night. The whole country turned out to look for him. He came back a year later, dumped on the doorstep of a police station in Ski, unharmed. Nobody was ever charged. He has been bolted down since.

Told to the little one · Askeladden and the golden princess

The Ash-lad's first job · a story for the plane

There were three brothers who set out to win the hand of the princess. The two older brothers were tall and clever and rode fine horses. Askeladden walked, and everyone laughed at him. On the road he found a dead magpie, an old axle-key, and half a broken nettle. His brothers laughed harder. When they reached the palace, the king said: "Whoever finds the princess a golden magpie who sings, opens the golden door of the tower, and cures her nettle-rash, will marry her." And the brothers stopped laughing. And Askeladden did all three, because he had bothered to pick up the small useless things. The moral, according to every Norwegian grandparent: keep your eyes open. The small useless thing will matter.
