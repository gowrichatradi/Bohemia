Day 5 · Fri 2 Oct · Reine — the far west

Stories, myths & legends

Out here the cliffs are older than any story anyone has. So the stories are older than most others — a sea-cave with dancing figures painted on the wall three thousand years ago, an entire village that was evacuated by boat in 1949 because the wind was too strong to leave in any other way, and a house spirit who will spoil your Christmas if you forget his butter.

The dancing figures three thousand years old

Kollhellaren cave · Refsvika

Deep inside a sea-cave near Refsvika, on a wall you can only reach by boat, are twenty-three little red stick figures with their arms raised as if dancing. Nobody knows who painted them. Nobody knows why. They were made in ochre and animal fat around 1000 BC, when the pyramids were already ancient, and they have been dancing on that wall for three thousand winters. Villagers used to leave a plate of dried fish above the cave mouth every midsummer, because the local story was that the figures came off the wall on the solstice, danced on the beach in the endless light, and were back on the rock by morning. Nobody eats the fish. But somebody keeps leaving it.

The village the wind emptied

Måstad · Værøy · abandoned 1949

Just south of here, on the island of Værøy, was a village called Måstad. For four hundred years its people lived on nothing but puffins. They caught them with dogs — a specific Norwegian dog with six toes on each foot, bred nowhere else on Earth, called the Lundehund — sent down into the cliff burrows to bring back one puffin at a time. The men climbed the cliffs on ropes. The women plucked the birds. That was the year. In 1949, after a series of storms that made the crossing impossible for weeks, the government quietly evacuated the last families. The empty houses are still there. So is the puffin colony. And there are still Lundehunds in the world — every single one descended from six survivors of a distemper epidemic in the 1960s.

The troll who prefers to sleep in

Reinebringen · 448m

Reinebringen — the toothy mountain over the village, the one every photograph of Reine is taken from — has a troll living inside it, according to the older families here. His name is Reinar and he is enormous and he is grumpy in the way only ancient stone can be. If you climb before sunrise, and you are quiet, you can hear him snoring through the rock, and you get clear weather as a reward. If you climb late and stomp about, you wake him, and by lunchtime there are clouds. Hikers who reach the summit and whisper a small apology at the cairn — unnskyld, Reinar — often get colour in the sky as they come down. Nobody insists you believe this. Everybody does it anyway.

The little man in the loft who wants butter

Nusfjord · every rorbu · every winter

Every red-painted fisherman's cabin in Lofoten has a nisse living in the loft. He is small, old, has a red pointed cap and four fingers on each hand, and he looks after the luck of the house. He asks very little. On Christmas Eve you put a bowl of porridge in the loft for him, with a lump of butter melting on top. He notices if the butter is missing. Nets tangle. Milk goes sour. Engines refuse to start on the coldest morning. Cabins that have stood empty for decades are said to still be fed, quietly, by families in Oslo who post packets of porridge to the caretaker with instructions in careful handwriting. Some traditions are so cheap to keep that nobody sees why you'd stop.

The boat that grew inside the tree

Nesland boatbuilders · Vestvågøy

The boatbuilders of Nesland made small clinker boats called faerings — the shape you see in every old photograph of a Lofoten fisherman leaning on his oars — and they were famous, for two hundred years, for the way their boats seemed to have been born rather than made. The local saying was that a Nesland boatbuilder did not cut a boat out of a tree; he found the boat already inside it, by listening. Old men walked the forest in autumn, tapping trunks with the back of a small hammer, listening for the one that gave the right note. That one became next year's boat. The last Nesland boatbuilder retired in 1998 and told a journalist that he still walks the forest, out of habit. He is looking for a boat that he has no longer any intention of building.

Told to the little one · The horse with two eyes forward

Norwegian fjord horse folklore

Norwegian fjord horses are small and stubby and pale gold, and they are one of the oldest kinds of horse in the world. The Vikings had them. Norwegian children are told that if you meet a fjord horse and it looks at you with both eyes at once — which means it has turned its whole face towards you — it likes you very much, and today will be a good day. If it looks at you with one eye it thinks you are alright. If it turns its back, go home and try again tomorrow. You are not a horse person yet.
