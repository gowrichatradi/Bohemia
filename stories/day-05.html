Day 5 · Fri 2 Oct · Reine — the far west

Stories, myths & legends

Out here the cliffs are older than any story you have. So the stories are older than most others — cave paintings that dance on midsummer, mountain trolls with a preferred sleeping hour, house-spirits who expect their porridge with butter.

The dancing figures in Kollhellaren cave

Refsvika · cave paintings ~3000 years old

In a sea-cave near Refsvika are 23 red stick figures painted on the wall about 3000 years ago — small, dancing, arms up. Nobody knows who painted them or why. Local legend says the figures leave the wall on midsummer's eve when the sun does not set, dance on the beach, and are back on the rock by morning. There is a scientific version of this — the ochre paint reflects a very particular slant of sun at solstice and appears to shift. The scientific version does not explain why the villagers of Refsvika, before they were relocated in 1949, kept a plate of dried fish above the cave-mouth every June.

Reinar, the troll of Reinebringen

Reinebringen · 448m

Reinebringen — the mountain you will hike — is (according to Lofoten folklore) home to a troll named Reinar. He is old and enormous and prefers to sleep late. Hikers who reach the summit before dawn are said to hear him snoring through the rock, and if they hurry back down before he wakes, they are rewarded with clear weather. Hikers who arrive after 10 a.m. wake him. He is grumpy, and clouds roll in. Sunrise hikers who bother to be polite — a whispered "sorry, Reinar" at the top — are said to get colour in the sky.

The nisse of every rorbu

Nusfjord · fishermen's cabins

Every rorbu (red-painted fisherman's cabin) in Lofoten has a nisse living in the loft — a small old man with a red pointed cap, four fingers on each hand, and a fierce loyalty to the household. He looks after the cabin's luck. In return, every Christmas Eve the family leaves him a bowl of porridge with a lump of butter melting on top. Forget the butter — even once — and he takes offence: milk goes off, nets tangle, a boat's engine won't start. Cabins that stand empty for years are said to be still fed by the last owner's descendants, who post packets of porridge in the mail with instructions to the caretaker.

Fossegrim of the Fredvang bridges

Fredvang · concrete bridges 1988

The Fredvang bridges sing when the wind is right — a hum through the steel that engineers explain with resonance. The locals prefer the older explanation: Fossegrim, the water-sprite who lives under waterfalls, has taken up residence under the bridge and plays a fiddle he made from a birch struck by lightning. If you leave him a white kid goat on a Thursday he will teach you the fiddle. A brown goat gets you only tuning. Do not try to leave him a chicken.

Nesland — the village where boats grow

Nesland · Vestvågøy

Nesland's boatbuilders were famous for wooden Nordland faerings — small clinker boats that seemed to be born rather than built. A local saying held that a Nesland boatbuilder didn't cut a boat from a tree; he found the boat inside the tree by listening for it. Old men in Nesland would walk in the forest tapping trunks with a hammer, waiting for one to answer with the right note. When it did, that was their next boat. The last Nesland boatbuilder retired in 1998. He said he still walks the forest, out of habit.

Told to the little one · The fjord horse

Fjord horse folklore

The stocky pale-gold Norwegian fjord horse is one of the oldest breeds in the world — the Vikings had them. Norwegian children are told that if you meet a fjord horse and it looks at you with both eyes at once, it likes you, and you will have a good day. If it looks at you with only one eye, it thinks you are alright but is unimpressed. If it turns its back, go home — you are not a horse person yet.
