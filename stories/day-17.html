Day 17 · Wed 14 Oct · Prague — the day the third night bought

Stories, myths & legends

A princess who prophesied Prague from a rock. Three silver rods a monk hid under his cloak. A saint whose statue turns her head. And one of the last wild horses of Central Asia, saved from extinction by a zoo in Prague.

Libuše's prophecy

Vyšehrad · c. 700 AD

The mother-founder of the Czech nation was Princess Libuše, said to have had the gift of prophecy. Standing on the rock at Vyšehrad she looked north across the river and said "I see a great city whose glory will touch the stars" — Prague. She was told to choose a husband, and dispatched a horse to find him unaided; the horse returned with a plowman named Přemysl, whom she married. Every subsequent Czech king traced back to him. The old bronze doors of the Vyšehrad basilica show the prophecy in relief. It happened here. Or at least, has always been said to have happened here, which in Prague is close enough.

The monk and the silver rods

Kutná Hora · origin story

Around 1260 a Cistercian monk was walking through a vineyard near the village of Sedlec and saw three thin rods of pure silver sprouting from the earth. He covered them with his monk's cloak — his kutna — to mark the spot and went to fetch the abbot. The Kutná Hora silver deposits, discovered on that spot, made Bohemia one of the richest kingdoms in medieval Europe and paid for a great many things you have seen. The town's name — Kutná Hora, "the cloak's mountain" — remembers the moment.

The saint who turns her head

St. Barbara's Cathedral · Kutná Hora

St. Barbara is the patron saint of miners — she blesses their descent, and legend says she warns them of danger. The cathedral in Kutná Hora is dedicated to her and was built by the mining guilds themselves. Miners of the 15th and 16th centuries said that the statue of Barbara over the mineshaft entrance would sometimes turn her face away as they arrived for the shift. Any miner who saw her face turned away that morning didn't descend. The mine records are full of days when a shift was suddenly light on labour and always for the "official" reason — a cold, a family matter — but the pattern of accidents in the shafts on those days is (according to Kutná Hora archivists) statistically odd.

The last wild horses

Prague Zoo · Przewalski's horse

The Przewalski's horse — the only truly wild horse still on Earth, distinct genetically from the domestic horse — went extinct in the wild by 1969. It was saved by a captive breeding programme centred on Prague Zoo, which had European stock going back to the 1930s. Every Przewalski's horse alive today, including reintroduced herds in Mongolia and the Chernobyl exclusion zone, is descended from 12 individual horses. Prague Zoo bred a large share of them. There is a small stubby foal at the zoo now who is descended from every one of the 12 horses that saved the species. It is possible to say that Prague brought back a species that had ended.

The garden nobody built for a general

Wallenstein Garden · Malá Strana · 1620s

Albrecht von Wallenstein was the Thirty Years' War's most feared general — commander of the imperial armies, and, in his own estimation, a man too grand to answer to the emperor. He built his palace and garden in Prague on land cleared of 26 houses and a monastery. The garden's centrepiece, the dripstone wall — a wall of grotesque cave-like stone faces — was built to no plan; his architect was told to hire stonemasons and let them "do what they wanted". If you look at the wall for long enough, more faces appear. Wallenstein was assassinated in 1634, on the emperor's orders, before the garden was finished. The wall is still there. Still adding faces, if you have time.

Told to the little one · Krtek the mole

Krtek · beloved Czech children's cartoon · 1957

Krtek is a small black mole with a red nose who lives in a burrow in the woods and gets into gentle adventures. He is Czech children's television's most beloved character — animated since 1957, silent (so translation was never needed, which is why every Central European child of a certain age also knows him). His creator, Zdeněk Miler, went to the Moon: a Krtek plush toy travelled on the space shuttle Endeavour in 2011. Point out Krtek on any bookshop shelf. There will be one.
