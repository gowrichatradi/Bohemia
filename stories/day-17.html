Day 17 · Wed 14 Oct · Prague — the day the third night bought

Stories, myths & legends

An astronomer with a nose of gold and a pet moose. An emperor who moved his entire court to Prague to be nearer to his alchemists and painters. A shy young mathematician in a rented room who worked out, in ink, the shape of the orbits that every spacecraft still follows today. And a small stubby foal at Prague Zoo who is descended from the very last twelve wild horses on Earth.

The astronomer with a nose of gold

Tycho Brahe · died in Prague · 24 October 1601

The greatest astronomer of the sixteenth century, Tycho Brahe, had a metal nose. He had lost the real one at twenty in a small mishap during a mathematics argument with a cousin, and for the rest of his life he wore a beautifully made replacement of a copper-gold alloy, held on with a little tin of glue that he kept on his belt. He also had, at one point, a pet moose — a real one — that had the run of his castle in Denmark and would follow him from room to room. He built his own observatory on his own island, with his own instruments, each one hand-forged and calibrated to a level of accuracy nobody had ever managed before. Over twenty years he measured the exact position of every visible star and every planet in the sky, night after night, to within one sixtieth of a degree — by eye. No telescope. Just patience, and instruments the size of small houses. When he moved to Prague at the invitation of Emperor Rudolf II, he brought all his notebooks with him. His last words, whispered to his shy young assistant, were let me not seem to have lived in vain. The assistant was Johannes Kepler. What Kepler did with those notebooks changed astronomy for ever.

The emperor who collected everything

Rudolf II · Prague Castle · court moved from Vienna 1583

Rudolf II, Holy Roman Emperor, moved his entire court from Vienna to Prague in 1583 for a single reason. He was fascinated by learning and he wanted to be surrounded by it. Every scientist, every painter, every inventor, every gifted weirdo in Europe with an interesting theory ended up eventually at his court. He hired John Dee and Edward Kelley the alchemists. He hired Tycho Brahe and, later, Johannes Kepler. He hired Giuseppe Arcimboldo, who painted portraits of Rudolf made entirely out of fruit and vegetables — a pear for a nose, a fig for a chin, a pumpkin for a cheek — which are now some of the most reproduced paintings of the Renaissance. Rudolf assembled what may have been the first proper museum in Europe: three thousand carefully catalogued objects — minerals, coins, mechanical automata that could bow to visitors, telescopes, globes, and the horn of a narwhal that everyone happily called a unicorn. He lodged his alchemists on a tiny street below the castle called Zlatá ulička — Golden Lane — and asked them, politely, if they might try to make him some gold. They never did. What they did instead was invent modern chemistry by accident. The lane is still there. It is very small. Every doorway is painted a different colour. Franz Kafka lived at number 22 for two years and wrote a book there.

Kepler's ellipses, worked out in a rented room

Johannes Kepler · Karlova Street · 1601–1609

When Tycho Brahe died, he left one thing behind that mattered more than his instruments or his gold nose. He left twenty years of hand-recorded planetary positions, more precise than any measurements anyone had ever made. Those notes went to his shy young assistant, Johannes Kepler. Kepler moved into a rented room on Karlova Street in Prague — a narrow street you have almost certainly walked down without noticing — and he sat there for the next eight years, alone, with the notebooks and a stack of paper and an inkpot, trying to work out the shape of Mars's orbit. Every scholar in history had assumed the planets moved in perfect circles. Kepler tried circles. They didn't fit Tycho's data. He tried ovals. They didn't fit either. Finally, on a quiet evening some time in 1605, he tried an ellipse — a stretched circle with two focal points instead of one. It fit. Perfectly. He wrote in the margin of his notes: I was as if awakened from a dream. That single equation — the ellipse — is the basis of how every spacecraft, from Voyager to the Mars rovers, is still navigated. NASA runs its trajectories on it. It was worked out with paper and ink in a room in Prague by a very shy man using data collected by a man with a metal nose. The house is still on Karlova Street.

The silver rods and the monk's cloak

Kutná Hora · c. 1260 · Cistercian monastery

Around 1260, a Cistercian monk was walking through a vineyard on the hill above the village of Sedlec, said his afternoon prayers, and lay down under a hedge to sleep. When he woke, three thin rods of pure silver were growing out of the soil beside his head. He was so afraid he had lost his mind that he covered them with his own robe — his kutna — to mark the spot, and ran to fetch the abbot. The rods were real. Below them, at fifty metres, was the largest silver deposit in medieval Europe. Within a generation, the tiny village had a mint that produced silver coins for most of Christendom, and Bohemia became one of the richest kingdoms on the continent. The Prague Cathedral, the Charles Bridge, and a dozen palaces you have walked past were paid for by the veins under that monk's robe. The town is still called Kutná Hora, which means the mountain of the cloak.

The last wild horse in the world

Prague Zoo · Przewalski's horse · saved from extinction

There is only one truly wild horse species on Earth. Every other horse — mustangs, brumbies, feral horses everywhere — is a domestic horse whose ancestors escaped. Only Przewalski's horse is a separate species that never was tamed. In 1969 it was declared extinct in the wild. The Mongolian steppes had none left. That should have been the end of the story. But a handful of Przewalski's horses were living in zoos, most notably at Prague Zoo, which had bred them since the 1930s. Prague took the lead on a captive breeding programme with twelve individuals. Twelve. Every Przewalski's horse alive today, in every zoo, and in the reintroduced wild herds now running again on the Mongolian steppe, and in the Chernobyl exclusion zone where a handful were released in 1998 and are quietly thriving, is descended from those twelve. There is a small stubby foal at Prague Zoo right now who is descended from every one of them. Prague brought back a species that had ended. It is the only city that has done this.

The wall of a thousand faces

Wallenstein Garden · Malá Strana · 1620s

Albrecht von Wallenstein was one of the most powerful generals in seventeenth-century Europe, and he decided he needed a palace in Prague to match his opinion of himself. He built a huge Baroque villa on the Malá Strana below the castle, and behind it he laid out an elaborate formal garden. For one particular wall in the garden, he gave the stonemasons an unusual instruction: build whatever you like, in whatever shape you like. Do not tell me what it will look like. Just make it. What they made is the dripstone wall — a long, strange artificial cliff of blistered, rippled, cave-like stone that, if you look at it for long enough, begins to reveal faces. A human face. A wolf. A child. An old man. Faces that were not there when you walked up. The garden is still open. The wall is unchanged. Stand in front of it for ten minutes. See how many faces come out to meet you.

Told to the little one · Krtek the little mole

Krtek · Czech children's cartoon since 1957

Krtek is a small black mole with a red nose who lives in a burrow in the woods, and every Czech child grows up watching his gentle little adventures on television. He has almost no words — he just makes little happy squeaks — which is why children in every country love him. His creator, Zdeněk Miler, worked on him for over fifty years. In 2011 a Krtek plush toy was carried into space on the Space Shuttle Endeavour by an astronaut whose grandmother was Czech. You will see Krtek in every Prague bookshop. Point him out. She will love him.
