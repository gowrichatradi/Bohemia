Day 9 · Tue 6 Oct · The Bergensbanen → Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

A railway that took thirty-four years to build across a mountain plateau nobody wanted to cross. A glacier that stood in for the ice planet Hoth in The Empire Strikes Back. A remote station where Amundsen practised the routines that got him to the South Pole. And a tunnel that turns in a complete circle inside the mountain.

Amundsen practised for the South Pole here

Finse · 1222m · highest station on the line

Halfway through the journey you pass the highest railway station in northern Europe — Finse, at twelve hundred and twenty-two metres. In winter Finse is buried in snow. It is treeless. It is white in every direction. Nothing here looks like it is on the same planet as the fjords you left this morning. And this is exactly why Roald Amundsen came here to practise for the South Pole. In 1909 and 1910, before his Antarctic expedition, Amundsen and his team spent whole winters at Finse with sled dogs — running the dogs, testing the sleds, calibrating their fuel and food loads, getting used to the whiteout. Finse was the closest thing to Antarctica anyone in Europe could reach by train. When he reached the South Pole in December 1911, thirty-four days before Scott, most of the routines he had used to do it had been rehearsed on the plateau you are crossing right now. The train stops at Finse for two minutes. Get off if you can. The air is the same air.

The railway the trolls kept breaking

Bergensbanen · surveyed 1875 · completed 1909

The Bergen line took thirty-four years to build. It crosses the Hardangervidda at over 1,200 metres, and at Finse it climbs so high that in some winters the train has been buried for days. The tunnellers were mostly farmers on short summer contracts, working with dynamite in weather that was frankly better suited to reindeer, and they wrote in the site logs — which the state railway has preserved — about tools that walked, sleeper timbers moved overnight, and lanterns snuffed with no wind. Foremen were required to write up "unaccountable interference" like any other work delay. The crews called the whole line trollbanen, the troll railway. When it opened in 1909, King Haakon travelled its length and remarked, drily, that it was the greatest thing his kingdom had built. It cost, in that year's kroner, the equivalent of a quarter of the entire national budget.

The glacier that pretended to be Hoth

Hardangerjøkulen · Finse · April 1979

The great white slope above Finse station, blindingly bright even on a grey day, is the Hardangerjøkulen glacier — and it played the ice planet Hoth in The Empire Strikes Back. The crew arrived from Elstree studios in April 1979 and spent a fortnight filming the wampa scene, the tauntaun, and Luke half- buried in the snow. It was the coldest shoot in the history of the franchise. When the storm hit on day three, director Irvin Kershner had Mark Hamill open the door of the hotel and deliver his lines into the blizzard, saving them a set. That was really the wind on Hoth. Glaciologists will tell you the glacier also "sings" — long low tones as ice cracks internally under pressure. The Finse herders used to say the sound was the voices of every herder who had died on the ice.

The train that turns a complete circle inside a mountain

Flåmsbana · Myrdal → Flåm · 20km, 866m descent

The branch line you switch to at Myrdal drops 866 metres to sea level in twenty kilometres. It is one of the steepest normal- gauge railways in the world. It has five separate braking systems because engineers, thoroughly, could not sleep at night. At one point the tunnel doubles back on itself and turns through 360 degrees inside the mountain, so that after five minutes in the dark you emerge and see the piece of valley you were looking at just before, only now it is a hundred metres below you. The engineer who surveyed the line in the 1920s, Hans Kolderup, is said to have sent a mountain goat ahead of his survey team through the crags with a small red flag tied to its horns — on the reasoning that if the goat could pick a path, a train could be persuaded to. There is a plaque at Myrdal station that mentions him. It does not mention the goat.

The reindeer spirit and the tobacco stone

Hardangervidda · Europe's largest wild reindeer herd

The plateau this train is crossing is home to about seven thousand wild reindeer — the largest herd in Europe. The old advice for a lost hiker on the Hardangervidda, if you find you can't see the hut you were meant to be walking to, is this: find a flat stone, place a pinch of tobacco on it, and speak aloud this is for the reindeer. Within an hour you should meet a lead animal. Walk in the direction the animal is walking, and you will come out at a shelter within a day. This was serious advice in the 1930s. Hikers carried tobacco specifically for it. Modern hikers carry GPS — but the older Norwegian walkers on the plateau still make the offering, quietly, as a courtesy.

Told to the little one · The kitchen nisse and the porridge

Norwegian house-spirit

Every old Norwegian house has a nisse in the pantry — a very small old man in a red pointy hat, only knee-high, who keeps the milk from spoiling and the mice out of the flour. You almost never see him. In return he wants one thing: a bowl of porridge with a great big yellow lump of butter melting on top, left out on Christmas Eve. If you forget the butter he sulks. If you forget the bowl he leaves. Nobody wants a house without a nisse. Ask her if she thinks the hotel might have one tonight.
