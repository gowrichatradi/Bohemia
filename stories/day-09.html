Day 9 · Tue 6 Oct · The Bergensbanen → Bergen

Stories, myths & legends

A train through mountains that took 34 years to build, past a glacier that stood in for Hoth, over a plateau where the reindeer spirit still leads lost hikers home if you have tobacco.

The troll railway

Bergensbanen · 1875–1909

The Bergen Line took thirty-four years to build across the Hardangervidda — dynamite through granite, at altitude, in weather that regularly killed workers. The tunnellers, mostly local farmers on short contracts, said trolls were stealing tools, moving sleeper timbers overnight, and blowing out lanterns. Foremen wrote in the site logs (still archived) about "unaccountable interference". The line was known during construction as trollbanen — the troll railway. Every kilometre of it is a small victory over whatever was in the rock.

Hardangerjøkulen sings

Finse · glacier · used as Hoth (1979)

The glacier above Finse doubled as Hoth for The Empire Strikes Back — the exterior scenes with Luke and the tauntaun were filmed on it in April 1979. Locals will tell you a stranger story: on midsummer's eve, when the sun barely dips, the glacier "sings" — long low tones as internal ice cracks under pressure. Glaciologists confirm the cracking. The old herders at Finse insisted the tones were the voices of previous herders who had died on the ice, greeting the new season. Either way it is worth stepping off the train to hear.

The reindeer spirit and the tobacco offering

Hardangervidda · Europe's largest mountain plateau

The Hardangervidda is home to Europe's largest wild reindeer herd — about 8,000 animals. Old hiking lore across the plateau holds that a lost hiker who lays a pinch of tobacco on a flat stone and speaks aloud "for the reindeer" will meet a lead animal within an hour who will walk in the direction of the nearest cabin. This was serious advice in the 1930s; hikers carried tobacco specifically for this. Modern hikers carry GPS. The offering, however, is still made — quietly — by older Norwegian walkers, as a courtesy rather than a strategy.

Vosa, the witch who tamed the falls

Voss · origin folklore

The town of Voss takes its name from the Old Norse voss, meaning roar — for the waterfalls that used to thunder through it. The origin story is that the first person to settle was a woman named Vosa, a witch who sang the falls to a lower volume so her children could sleep. She lived so long the villagers eventually forgot she was not a saint. When the church was built in 1277 they wanted to name it for her, but the bishop objected. The falls are still there, and quieter than the mountain would suggest.

The Flåm branch and the goat surveyor

Myrdal → Flåm · 20km, 866m descent

The Flåmsbana was surveyed in the 1920s across some of the steepest railway grades in Europe. The engineer in charge, Hans Kolderup, used to send his lead surveyor's goat ahead of the survey team through the mountains, on the reasoning that if the goat could get across, a train could be persuaded to. The goat's name has not survived. The line the goat walked is one of the great scenic railways of the world. Locals still tip their caps at the tunnel entrances "to the goat".

Told to the little one · The kitchen nisse

Norwegian house-spirit

Every old Norwegian house has a nisse in the pantry — a very small man in a red pointed cap who keeps the milk from spoiling and the mice from the flour. He is invisible. In return he wants a bowl of porridge with butter left out on Christmas Eve. If he doesn't get it, the milk spoils and the mice come back. Explain this to her over dinner. Watch her check the corners.
