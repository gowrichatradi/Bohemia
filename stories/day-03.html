Day 3 · Wed 30 Sep · Denmark → Reine

Stories, myths & legends

You cross the Arctic Circle today, and the stories change species. Down south it is kings and architects. Up here it is a whirlpool that swallows whales, a mountain with a arrow-hole punched clean through it, and a sun that will not rise for two months in winter and will not set for two months in summer. You are in a country that is on a different calendar from the sky.

The whirlpool that eats whales

The Moskstraumen · off Moskenesøya

Between the last two islands in the Lofoten chain, where the tide races through a narrow strait, the sea forms one of the strongest whirlpools on Earth — the Moskstraumen. Whales have been dragged into it. Fishing boats vanish there every century or so. The Norse believed it was made by two enormous magic millstones on the sea floor that would not stop grinding. Edgar Allan Poe wrote a horror story about it. Jules Verne dropped Captain Nemo into it at the end of Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea. Ferries pass within sight of it today, and locals will point without wanting to look at it too long. It is the reason the word "maelstrom" exists.

The seven sisters and the arrow through the mountain

Origin myth · Nordland coast

In the old story the coast north of Trondheim was full of giants. A giant called Hestmannen — the horseman — fell for a mountain- maiden called Lekamøya, the youngest of seven sisters, and one night he mounted his stone horse and rode her down. The sisters ran for their lives. Hestmannen fired an arrow to bring them down. Another giant, the King of Sømna, threw his hat in the way to save them. Then the sun came up, and everyone turned to stone where they stood — arrow, horse, hat and all. Every jagged peak you see from the E10 is one of them, frozen mid-chase. And if you drive south to Brønnøysund you will pass Torghatten, a mountain with a hole 160 metres long punched clean through it — that is the arrow. Still in flight.

The two months of no sun and the two months of no night

Lofoten · above 68° north

You have just crossed the Arctic Circle. That is a line drawn by geometry, not by anyone standing here — the line above which, for at least one full day each year, the sun does not rise. In Reine the sun disappears completely for two whole months every winter, from around 8 December to 4 January. It is not dark like night. The sky at midday goes a slow, deep, luminous blue, as if the whole village were inside a glass bowl of ink, and the mountains hold that colour for hours. Locals call it the blue hour. Then, in perfect balance, from May to July the sun does not set at all. You can read a newspaper on a park bench at three in the morning. Children in Lofoten grow up entirely used to the fact that on their birthday the sun might not come up, and that in summer they can play football at midnight. It is one of the few places on the planet where the sky is on a completely different calendar from the clocks.

Sildpollnes — the church built by a promise

Sildpollnes church · 1891

A fisherman was caught out in a bad storm in the sound. Waves black as basalt. Rain like a sheet of iron. He shouted a promise into it — the kind of promise you make when you don't expect to live to keep it — that if God delivered him alive to a piece of dry land, he would build a church on the exact spot. He washed up on the narrow spit at Sildpollnes. No village. No road. No reason for a building there at all. He built it anyway. It's still there — small, white, alone on a splinter of land, five minutes off the E10 — and the wind moves through it as if reading the terms.

The Trollfjord battle that fishermen won

Trollfjord · 6 March 1890

The Trollfjord is a knife-slit two kilometres long, walls of black rock closing to a hundred metres across. In the spring of 1890 the cod ran into it in immense numbers, and a fleet of new steam trawlers arrived to net the entire mouth shut and take the whole run. The local fishermen, in open rowboats, refused to leave. On 6 March the trawlers turned fire-hoses on them. The fishermen fought back with axes, oars, boat-hooks and stones. It ended with the trawlers driven back and a new Norwegian law that banned the netting of a fjord's mouth by industrial boats. Locals will tell you the trolls in the cliffs above helped. The law didn't need their signature, but nobody has ruled it out.

Told to the little one · The Northern Lights are foxes

Sámi folklore

In the Sámi story that is told all across the north, the Northern Lights are made by an enormous arctic fox running along a snowy ridge. His tail sweeps the snow up into the air and it catches the starlight and turns green and pink as it falls. The faster he runs the brighter the sky. Sometimes he stops to sleep, and there are no lights at all. If she looks up tonight and doesn't see them, tell her the fox is having a nap.
