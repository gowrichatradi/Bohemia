Day 3 · Wed 30 Sep · Denmark → Reine

Stories, myths & legends

You cross the Arctic Circle today, and the folklore changes accent. Down south they tell you about kings and architects. Up here it's giants who fell in love, and mountains that were once seven sisters.

The Seven Sisters and the giant Hestmannen

Origin myth · Nordland coast

Long ago the coast was populated by giants. Hestmannen — the horseman-giant — fell in love with Lekamøya, the youngest of seven sister-maidens on the island of Leka, and one night he mounted his stone horse and rode after them. The sisters ran north for their lives all night. As the first sun rose over the horizon, Hestmannen fired an arrow to bring them down — but the sun caught them all, and the arrow, and the giant, and turned the whole chase to stone. Every jagged peak you see from the E10 today is one of them, frozen mid-flight. The mountain "Torghatten", further south, has a hole through it — Hestmannen's arrow, still in the air.

The Trollfjord's echo

Trollfjord · Raftsundet

The Trollfjord is 2km long, 100m wide at its mouth, and closes to walls of black rock. Fishermen who rowed in alone in the old days said a troll would follow behind them, mimicking every stroke of their oars — perfectly, delayed by a second, so you could never quite tell if you were being followed or hearing yourself. The safe thing was to row with another boat. In 1890 the fjord was the site of a real battle between local fishermen in open boats and a fleet of steam trawlers trying to net the whole entrance shut. The fishermen won. Some say the troll helped.

Sildpollnes — the church a storm chose

Sildpollnes church · 1891

A fisherman caught in a terrible storm made a promise to God: build a church on the first spot of land his boat touches, alive. He washed up on the narrow spit at Sildpollnes — a place with no village, no road, no reason. He built the church there anyway. It's a five-minute walk from a lay-by on the E10 and there is still no village. The wind moves through it as if approving.

Reine and the sun as gift

Reine · Moskenesøya

Reine sits under 700-metre cliffs. It gets a lot of rain and, in winter, no direct sun for two months at all. The name is Norse for "clean" or "clear" and the local story is that clear days at Reine are not weather — they are gifts from Freya, who visits when the mountain trolls are asleep. The saying is "En dag med sol i Reine er en dag Freya sover ikke" — a sunny day at Reine is a day Freya isn't sleeping. Take your photos accordingly.

Svolvær and the goat that guards

Svolværgeita · 569m

Above Svolvær is a rock formation with two horns — the "Svolvær Goat". Local legend says it's the last of a herd of goats a witch turned to stone for eating her garden. Climbers who jump between the two horns (there is a 1.5m gap over a 150m drop, and yes people do it) claim the stone is warm to the touch on the coldest days, because the goat is still trying to bleat.

Told to the little one · The Northern Lights are foxes

Sámi folklore

In the Sámi story, the aurora is made by revontulet — fox-fires. A great arctic fox runs across the snow and his tail sweeps sparks into the sky. The faster he runs the greener the sky. Some nights he stops to sleep, and there are no lights at all. If she asks tonight where the fox went, tell her he's resting.
