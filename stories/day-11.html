Day 11 · Thu 8 Oct · Bergen → Aurlandsfjord

Stories, myths & legends

A fjord named after a sea god. A church so small the goats built it. Thirteen hairpins the villagers built with picks after Napoleon's engineers refused. A viewing platform where the world briefly looks like it was designed by someone in a hurry.

Njord's fjord

Nærøyfjord · UNESCO · 250m at its narrowest

Nærøyfjord takes its name from Njord, the Norse god of the sea, wind, and safe voyages. He was one of the older gods — a Vanir, not an Aesir — and married briefly to the giantess Skadi, goddess of skiing. The marriage failed, because Njord could not sleep in her mountains for the howling of wolves, and Skadi could not sleep in his fjord for the crying of gulls. They separated amicably. Locals will tell you Njord still visits the fjord in summer, and Skadi visits in winter on skis; on autumn days like today, both are said to overlap for a few weeks. It is the polite explanation for the weather.

Undredal's goat church

Undredal stave church · 1147

Undredal is a village of 80 people and 500 goats, and the church seats 40. The founding story: a storm broke over Undredal in the 12th century and every goat in the village bolted uphill and huddled around a single flat rock. When the storm passed the herders went to fetch them and found the goats would not move. The village took this as instruction and built the church on that rock. The building is still there, whitewashed, with a small wooden steeple. The goats still outnumber the pews.

Napoleon's engineers said no

Stalheimskleiva · 13 hairpins · 1846

The old post road from Bergen up to Stalheim climbs 13 hairpins in 1.5km — a 20% grade past two waterfalls. Napoleon's engineers, brought north during the Napoleonic Wars to survey Norwegian mountain routes, are said to have refused this pass as "impossible for wheeled vehicles". Fifty years later local villagers built it themselves, without engineers, without dynamite in useful quantities, and largely with picks. Cars still drive it (one-way downhill only now). Every hairpin is a tiny insult to French military engineering.

Fossegrim under Tvindefossen

Tvindefossen · roadside waterfall

Fossegrim, the water-sprite behind Norwegian waterfalls, is a fiddle-player of supernatural skill. The old bargain: offer him a white kid goat on a Thursday night in mid-summer, and he'll teach you the fiddle until your bow hand bleeds. Any other offering (a chicken, a lesser goat, a Tuesday) gets you tuning. Ole Bull, the great Bergen violinist (1810–1880), was said by his contemporaries to have made the deal — he played with a sound "impossibly clean", refused to explain his practice regime, and always avoided Thursdays in mid-summer.

Stegastein's sightline

Stegastein · 650m above Aurlandsfjord · 2006

The Stegastein platform juts 30m out over the void, ending in a glass wall so clear you don't quite trust it. The architects — Todd Saunders and Tommie Wilhelmsen — designed it so that at the far end the visitor experiences a small internal lurch: it looks like the platform ends in open sky. The story around the village is that the wall was originally proposed as no wall at all, and that a local goat herder walked out to the end during construction, spat, and said "no". A wall was added. The lurch, however, is still there.

Told to the little one · The nissen and the porridge

Norwegian Christmas folklore

On Christmas Eve every farm in Norway leaves a bowl of porridge for the nisse, the small red-capped farm-spirit who looks after the animals all year. One year a boy on a farm ate the butter off the top of the porridge before setting it out. The nisse was so insulted he moved the barn cat's tail onto the family dog and vice versa. The boy had to apologise with a new bowl of porridge and extra butter before the animals were themselves again. Do not skimp on the butter.
