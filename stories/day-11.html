Day 11 · Thu 8 Oct · Bergen → Aurlandsfjord

Stories, myths & legends

A road so ridiculous that Napoleon's engineers took one look and refused to build it, so the villagers built it themselves with picks. A tunnel under a mountain built with the wrong exit so you come out five kilometres from where the map said. And a village of eighty people and five hundred goats, where the goats — during a storm — decided where the church was going.

The world's longest road tunnel and the light in the middle

Lærdal Tunnel · 24.5 km · opened 2000

You will drive today through the longest road tunnel in the world. Twenty-four and a half kilometres straight through a mountain. The Norwegians who built it knew that people go a bit strange inside a tunnel that takes twenty minutes to cross — claustrophobic, sleepy, unmoored in time — so they carved three enormous vaulted rest chambers into the mountain at rough intervals of five kilometres. Each is lit blue at the walls and pale yellow overhead, so that when you drive into one it looks uncannily like coming out into a false dawn under an open sky. Cars slow down. Nobody who has driven through it forgets the moment the blue light appears. It is one of the great pieces of human landscape design of the last century, and almost no traveller outside Norway has heard of it.

The road Napoleon's engineers said no to

Stalheimskleiva · 13 hairpins · 1846

The old post road up from the valley climbs thirteen hairpins in one and a half kilometres past two waterfalls, at a gradient of one in five. During the Napoleonic wars, French military engineers were brought north to survey Norwegian mountain passes. The Stalheim pass they refused outright — impossible for wheeled vehicles, they wrote. Fifty years later the local villagers, who could not afford to wait for anyone's opinion, simply built it themselves. No engineers. Very little dynamite. Picks, mostly, and shovels, and the memory of a French document that said it could not be done. Cars still drive it, one-way downhill, in summer. Every hairpin is a small polite insult to French military opinion.

The church the goats chose

Undredal · population 80 · goats 500

Undredal is a village that would fit on a postage stamp — eighty people, five hundred goats, one small white stave church that seats forty, and a shed where they make a caramel-brown goats'- milk cheese so beloved of Norwegian breakfast tables that the village supplies most of the country. The founding story of the church is the one told at the door of any tour. In the twelfth century a great storm broke over Undredal, and every goat in the village bolted up the hill and huddled around one particular flat rock. The storm passed. The herders went to fetch them. The goats refused to move. The villagers took this as instruction and built the church on that rock in 1147. It is still there. The goats still outnumber the pews by more than twelve to one, and they still walk through the village every evening in a small confident procession as if they own it. They do.

The fiddler who made a bargain

Fossegrim under Tvindefossen · Ole Bull · 1810–1880

Behind every real Norwegian waterfall lives a Fossegrim — a water-sprite who plays a fiddle so beautifully that anyone who hears him weeps and forgets which century they are in. The old bargain is well known: offer him a white kid goat on a Thursday night in midsummer, and he will teach you the fiddle for as long as your bow hand can bleed. A brown goat gets you tuning. A chicken gets you nothing. Ole Bull, the great Bergen violinist of the nineteenth century, was said by his own rivals to have made the deal. His playing was described as "impossibly clean". He refused to explain his practice regime. He avoided all engagements on Thursdays in midsummer. He never confirmed the story. He never denied it either.

The platform that lets you fall

Stegastein · 650m above Aurlandsfjord · 2006

The Stegastein platform juts thirty metres out over the void above Aurlandsfjord and ends in a glass wall so clear that from a distance it seems not to be there. The two architects, Todd Saunders and Tommie Wilhelmsen, designed it precisely to produce a small internal lurch — a fraction of a second, as you walk to the end, in which your body thinks the platform is about to run out over air. The design was almost approved without any glass at all. The village story is that a local goat herder, walking out to the end during construction, spat once over the edge, watched it fall, and said, calmly, nei. The glass was added the following week. The lurch, however, is still there. Stand still at the end for ten seconds and see if your feet believe the glass.

Told to the little one · Do not forget the butter

Norwegian Christmas folklore · again

There is a story every Norwegian child knows. One Christmas Eve a boy on a farm was setting out the porridge bowl for the nisse — the small old man in the red hat who looks after the animals — and he was hungry, and he ate the big lump of butter on top before he put the bowl in the pantry. The nisse was so insulted that in the night he swapped the tail of the cat with the tail of the dog. The boy had to leave a fresh bowl of porridge, with double butter, before either animal was itself again. She should never forget the butter. Nor should you.
