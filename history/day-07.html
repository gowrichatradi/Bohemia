Day 7 · Sun 4 Oct · Aurora day — Polar Park or Abisko

Under the driest sky in Scandinavia

A day designed around one thing that happens after dark. If Lofoten was clouded out behind you, this is the reason: Abisko, on the Swedish side, has the clearest statistical aurora sky in the Nordic countries — and if Polar Park did not fit yesterday, this morning is when you do it.

Did you knowAbisko sits inside a rain shadow cast by the Norwegian coastal mountains — the Atlantic weather dumps its water on Narvik and arrives at Torneträsk almost dry. The result is 250 clear-sky nights a year, more than any other populated place inside the Arctic Circle. NASA has used it as a control site for aurora research since the 1960s.

Abisko National Park

Established 1909 · one of Sweden's first · 77 km²

Abisko was gazetted in 1909, in the same wave of legislation that gave Sweden its first nine national parks — the Nordic countries invented modern national-park law between them, twenty years before most of Europe caught up. The park was routed around the small station and the STF Turiststation hotel, which have been there since the railway (the Malmbanan iron-ore line to Narvik) was completed in 1902.

Notice the trailhead behind the Turiststation: this is the northern end of the Kungsleden, the King's Trail, which runs 440 km south to Hemavan through the Swedish mountains. People walk the whole thing in five weeks.

The Aurora Sky Station

Opened 1998 · 900 m above sea level · chairlift access

The chairlift up Mount Nuolja was originally a small ski lift built in the 1960s. In 1998 the STF opened the summit hut as a dedicated aurora-viewing station — a wooden building, a warm room, panoramic north-facing glass, and a small café that serves reindeer stew. It runs evenings 21:00–01:00 in October, one chair every ten seconds up, a 15-minute ride, and dress for the wind at 900 m even if the valley is calm.

Book online if you know you are going — the chairlift sells timed tickets and a strong-KP-forecast Saturday night sells out by mid-afternoon.

Lights Over Lapland · Chad Blakley

Aurora-photography workshops since 2010

Chad Blakley moved to Abisko in 2010 to photograph the aurora full-time and has been the resident aurora photographer ever since — his images run in National Geographic, BBC and every aurora book printed. His company runs nightly guided photo tours out of the Turiststation; if you have the camera and no idea what you're doing, this is the two hours worth spending.

Notice his prints and stickers in the STF gift shop and at the Sky Station — sold nowhere else.

Kristian Birkeland's aurora, seen in the wild

Charged particles · Earth's magnetic funnel · 100–300 km altitude

The Norwegian physicist proved in 1908 that the aurora is charged particles from the sun funnelled by Earth's magnetic field into the upper atmosphere. Green light (dominant at Abisko latitudes) comes from oxygen atoms at 100–150 km; red is oxygen at 200–300 km; the rare purple is nitrogen. If you see all three in one night, that is a strong storm, and you should stop trying to photograph it and just watch.

The Sámi and the northern lights

Guovssahasat · a spirit taboo

In Northern Sámi the aurora is guovssahasat — "the lights you can hear". The traditional taboo is not to wave, whistle or call at them, because they will come down and take you. The old rules were serious: a Sámi guide would not photograph the aurora in the 1930s because a flash was too much like calling. This is a story worth telling the little one, not a rule you have to follow.

Rombaksbotn & the sunken destroyers

Battles of Narvik · April–June 1940

The fjord finger running east from Narvik holds the wrecks of nine German destroyers scuttled or sunk in the Second Battle of Narvik on 13 April 1940 — one of the Kriegsmarine's worst days of the war. The wrecks are still there, in 40–200 m of water, and diveable in summer. The E10 west out of Riksgränsen runs directly along the north shore of Rombaksbotn; on a clear afternoon it is the emptiest scenic drive on this route.

Told to the little one · Reindeer bells

A Sámi story · every kid asks about the reindeer

Sámi reindeer wear small wooden bells around their necks, one per animal, all slightly different in tone — so the herder knows which reindeer is where in the fog without seeing it. The old story is that the bells were a gift from the reindeer themselves, so they would never be lost from the herd, and so their children would always know how to find them. If you hear one clonking in the woods above Abisko, that is why.
