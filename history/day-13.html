Day 13 · Sat 10 Oct · Bergen → Munich → Neuschwanstein

Ludwig's fantasy

Neuschwanstein is a 19th-century idea of the Middle Ages, built by a king who left everyday reality behind. It's why the Disney castle looks the way it does.

Did you knowLudwig II never lived to see Neuschwanstein finished. Two days after he was declared insane by his cabinet and confined to Berg Palace, he was found dead in shallow water on Lake Starnberg with his psychiatrist. Official verdict: suicide. Almost nobody believes it. He is buried in Munich and remains, 140 years later, Bavaria's most popular monarch — his birthday still marks a public holiday of sorts in some villages.

Schloss Neuschwanstein

Built 1869–1886 · commissioned by King Ludwig II of Bavaria

Ludwig II — Bavaria's "fairy-tale king" — commissioned Neuschwanstein as a personal retreat from public life, drawing on Wagner's operas (particularly Lohengrin and Tannhäuser) and a romanticised idea of a medieval castle. The building was designed by a theatre-set painter, Christian Jank, not a trained architect. Ludwig lived in it for only 172 days before he was declared insane and drowned mysteriously in 1886. It was opened to the public seven weeks after his death.

Speciality Walt Disney visited in 1935 and again in 1957; Sleeping Beauty Castle at Disneyland is directly modelled on Neuschwanstein, and the Cinderella castle at Walt Disney World echoes it. Roughly 1.4 million people visit each year.

Marienbrücke (Mary's Bridge)

Iron bridge 1866 · originally 1845 as timber

The bridge over the Pöllat Gorge was first built in 1845 by Ludwig's father Maximilian II, replaced with iron in 1866, and named for Ludwig's mother Marie. Ludwig positioned Neuschwanstein specifically so the bridge would give the frontal view he wanted photographed and painted. Every classic Neuschwanstein postcard is shot from Marienbrücke.

Notice the bridge is free, has no ticket and no opening hours — but it closes without warning in ice or high wind, which is a real risk in mid-October.

Schloss Hohenschwangau

Rebuilt 1832–1837 by Ludwig's father

The yellow castle in the valley below was Ludwig's childhood home. Maximilian II rebuilt it in Gothic Revival style from a medieval ruin in the 1830s, and Ludwig grew up here surrounded by murals of German legend — the same iconography he later commissioned at Neuschwanstein. His childhood room, with a painted-star ceiling, still exists.

Speciality Hohenschwangau is often skipped by day-tourists rushing to Neuschwanstein, but it is the older, lived-in castle and the one Ludwig knew first. It shaped the fantasy above it.

Alpsee

Glacial lake · 62 m deep · under Hohenschwangau

The Alpsee is a small, deep, turquoise glacial lake right below Hohenschwangau. Ludwig swam here as a child and rowed on it as a young king. A flat shore path runs the full circuit — about an hour — and the water is a distinct blue-green because of the fine glacial silt suspended in it.

Notice the view of Hohenschwangau reflected in the Alpsee, from the south shore path, is a photograph almost no coach tourist takes.

Füssen & Lechfall

Roman origins · timber floating on the Lech since medieval times

Füssen was a Roman way-station (Foetibus) on the Via Claudia Augusta. Its economic history since the Middle Ages ran on timber and lute-making — Füssen was the centre of German lute production from the 15th to the 17th century. Lechfall, on the town edge, is a narrow limestone gorge with a footbridge over the drop where the Lech gathers speed.

Notice Lechfall's rock walls preserve traces of the timber-floating chutes that carried logs down the Lech to the Danube for centuries.

Wallgau & Mittenwald

Wallgau: Lüftlmalerei tradition · Mittenwald: violin making since 1684

Both villages sit on the Isar in a valley between the Karwendel and the Wetterstein. Wallgau is a small hamlet famous for Lüftlmalerei — the pastel facade murals painted onto Upper Bavarian houses, a technique that peaked in the 18th century. Mittenwald has made violins since Matthias Klotz returned from Cremona around 1684 having studied with Nicola Amati — the Klotz family has been building instruments there ever since.

Speciality the Mittenwald violin-making tradition produced instruments comparable to Cremona for a period in the 17th–18th centuries. The town still runs a violin-making school (est. 1858) and stringed instruments are still built there today.

On screen · Neuschwanstein & Bavaria

Sleeping Beauty (and every Disney castle since)

1959 · Walt Disney

Walt Disney visited Neuschwanstein twice — in 1935 and 1957 — and the castle in the closing shot of Sleeping Beauty is a direct copy. Every Disney park castle since is a variation. If you're standing on the Marienbrücke, you are standing where the Disney concept artists stood.

Chitty Chitty Bang Bang

1968 · Ken Hughes

Neuschwanstein plays "Vulgaria" — the villainous baron's castle where children are forbidden. The exterior shots are all here. The Baron's grand hall is a set, but the establishing shots are Neuschwanstein at every angle.

Ludwig

1972 · Luchino Visconti · Helmut Berger

Visconti's four-hour opera about Ludwig II. Filmed on location at Neuschwanstein, Hohenschwangau, Linderhof and Herrenchiemsee. Sumptuous, decadent, occasionally excruciating — but if you want to understand the man who built the castle, this is the one.

The Great Escape

1963 · John Sturges

Not filmed here, but relevant: the Alpine chase sequence at the end — Steve McQueen on the motorbike — uses the same Bavarian mountain roads and villages as your drive to Krün tonight. Füssen, Mittenwald and Garmisch all appear. Some of the actual "Great Escape" tunnels were dug at Stalag Luft III far to the north-east.

The Sound of Music

1965 · Robert Wise · Julie Andrews

The opening "hills are alive" shot was filmed on the meadow near Werfen, Austria — an hour and a half east of Krün, on your route tomorrow. Two-day rain delay ran the crew's budget over; Wise finally shot it in the two clear hours before the next front closed in.
