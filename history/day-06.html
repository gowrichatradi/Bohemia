East along Lofoten — Vikings, glass, and the beach

A drive back east, with the two things you saved for the way: an 83-metre reconstructed longhouse and a glassblowing hamlet.

Lofotr Viking Museum, Borg

Longhouse foundation discovered 1981 · reconstruction opened 1995

In 1981 a farmer at Borg on Vestvågøy noticed unusual soil patterns while ploughing. The site turned out to be the largest Viking longhouse ever found — 83 metres by nine — belonging to a Norse chieftain around the 5th–10th centuries. The reconstruction rebuilt it on the same footprint, and the museum runs it as a living site: fires lit, costumed staff, blacksmithing and weaving.

Speciality the chieftain who lived here almost certainly connected to the same trading network as the Vikings who settled Iceland, the Faroes and Greenland. The gold foil "guldgubber" figurines found at Borg are among the finest Norse art recovered anywhere.

Haukland & Uttakleiv beaches

Vestvågøy · connected by a mountain tunnel opened 2003

Haukland and Uttakleiv sit either side of the Mannen ridge and were connected by a single-lane tunnel only in 2003 — before which Uttakleiv, in particular, was a hard-to-reach farming and fishing community. Both beaches face north-west and have been listed among the best in Europe.

Notice at Uttakleiv, the "Lofoten's eye" — a natural circular rock formation on the north side of the bay where sea has hollowed a bowl in the granite. And the drying racks at the head of the beach — still in use each winter.

Vikten Glasshytta

Founded 1974 · Åsvar Tangrand

Åsvar Tangrand — a glassblower who had trained in Sweden — built the first glass workshop north of the Arctic Circle here at Vikten in 1974. The hamlet has six houses; the workshop is one of them. His son now runs it, and the furnace has burned continuously since the 1970s.

Speciality Vikten glass is unusually thick-walled and heavy, a style Tangrand developed for Arctic conditions and the local light. It is unlike anything from the mainland tradition.

Unstad

Surf beach since the 1960s

In 1963 two locals — Thor Frantzen and Hans Egil Krane — began surfing at Unstad on homemade boards, having seen the sport in California. It was Norway's first surf spot and remains its most famous. The Unstad Arctic Surf camp runs year-round, wetsuits included.

Speciality October is the start of the good northern-Norway surf season — the North Atlantic swell lifts and the crowds have gone. Aurora over surfers is a documented photograph, not a fantasy.

Eggum

Coastal path · WW2 radar ruins

Eggum is a small farming community facing the open Norwegian Sea. Above it stand the concrete ruins of a German coastal radar station from 1943, part of the Atlantic Wall's northern reach. The Head sculpture — Markus Raetz's rotating steel piece — was placed here in 1992 on the coastal walk as part of the Artscape Nordland project.

Notice the coastal walk from Eggum to Unstad follows an old bridle path, still used by farmers moving sheep. About two hours one way if you have the day.
