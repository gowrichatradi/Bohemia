Day 10 · Wed 7 Oct · Bergen

Bergen — the Hanseatic city

For four hundred years Bergen was Norway's biggest city and Northern Europe's cod capital. The wooden buildings you're photographing are what remains of a German merchant colony.

Did you knowBergen has been Norway's rainiest city for decades — it holds the European record for consecutive days of rain (85, in 2006–2007). Locals sell umbrellas as tourist souvenirs, and the university offers a real course in "Hordaland weather resilience". If you get through a full day without rain, buy a lottery ticket.

Bryggen

UNESCO 1979 · Hanseatic wharf 14th–16th c. · current buildings post-1702 fire

Bryggen — literally "the wharf" — was the base of the German Hanseatic League's Bergen office (Kontor) from 1360 to 1754. German merchants controlled the stockfish trade from here, living in wooden longhouses running back from the wharf. Fires have destroyed and rebuilt Bryggen many times; most of what stands is from after the 1702 fire, but on the same medieval plot lines. Roughly a quarter of the original buildings survive.

Speciality the timber alleyways behind the front row — Schøtstuene, Bredsgården, Bugården — hold small workshops (leather, jewellery, ceramics) inside the old merchant halls. Free to walk through.

Bergen fish market (Fisketorget)

Trading since the 1200s · roofed hall 2012

The market at the head of the harbour has existed since at least the 13th century — it's mentioned in documents when Bergen was Norway's royal capital. Until 2012 it was entirely outdoor; the current glass-roofed Mathallen combines the modern hall with the surviving outdoor stalls. The catch on sale is what the fjord fleet landed that morning.

Notice the indoor hall is open year-round; the outdoor stalls shrink to a handful in the winter. Fresh king crab and langoustines are the market's speciality when in season.

Fløibanen funicular

Opened 15 January 1918 · Mount Fløyen 320 m

The Fløibanen has run since 1918. It was rebuilt with new Swiss cars in 2002 but the tunnel and infrastructure are the original, and it is one of the oldest cable railways still operating in Northern Europe. At the top: a viewpoint, a family-friendly troll-forest walk, and — since the 2010s — a small population of resident goats used for grazing.

Notice the ride is short — six minutes to the top station at 320 m — but it climbs a 26% gradient, one of the steepest passenger funiculars in the world.

Nordnes & Skuteviken

Wooden house districts · 17th–19th c. · largely spared 1916 fire

Both Nordnes (the peninsula west of Bryggen) and Skuteviken (behind Bryggen to the north) are neighbourhoods of white-clapboard timber houses on steep lanes, mostly built in the 18th and 19th centuries. They survived the great 1916 fire that destroyed much of central Bergen, and are the only remaining large area of pre-fire wooden housing still lived in.

Speciality Nordnes has the highest concentration of listed timber houses in Norway. Some are only reachable by staircases, not roads — a legacy of the 17th-century street plan.

Troldhaugen

Edvard Grieg's villa · built 1885 · museum since 1928

Edvard Grieg — Norway's national composer — built the villa in 1885 and wrote most of his later work in a small hut on the edge of the property overlooking the lake. He lived there each summer until his death in 1907. The house and hut are preserved as he left them, and lunchtime piano recitals run in a modern concert hall built into the slope beside.

Notice the composing hut has been left with pencil, glasses and manuscript scores on the desk. Grieg is buried in a cliff niche above the lake.

Fantoft Stave Church

Original c. 1150 · burned 1992 · reconstruction 1997

Fantoft was moved here in 1883 from its original site at Fortun, one of many stave churches saved by relocation as they fell out of local use. In June 1992 the church was burned to the ground by an act of arson connected to Norway's black-metal scene — one of the first of a wave of church burnings that followed. The current building is a faithful reconstruction from 1997.

Notice the reconstruction was hand-built using traditional stavework methods; even the roof shingles were made by axe rather than saw. The photograph of the burnt shell became the cover of Burzum's Aske EP.

For the little one

The three-year-old's version of today

Fløibanen up, troll walk at the top, goats. It's an easy 90 minutes that ticks 'Bergen with a child' entirely. If it's raining hard, Fjåk Chocolate on Bryggen has a small hot-chocolate counter and the room is warm.

For the drive · The Rest Is History — Hanseatic League

podcast · Holland & Sandbrook

The Hanseatic merchants built Bryggen. There's an episode on medieval trade networks that makes the wooden alleyways make sense. Roughly 90 minutes.

On screen · Bergen

Max Manus: Man of War

IMDb 7.2 · 2008 · Joachim Rønning & Espen Sandberg

The story of the Norwegian resistance saboteur. Filmed in Oslo but with Bergen's docks doubling as WWII port scenes — Bryggen's wooden warehouses passed for wartime Kristiansand.

Insomnia

IMDb 7.2 · 1997 · Erik Skjoldbjærg

The Norwegian original of the film Nolan later remade with Al Pacino. Northern-Norway scenes include several around the Bergen fjord — a detective without sleep in eternal summer daylight, unable to tell reality from hallucination.

The Wave (Bølgen)

IMDb 6.6 · 2015 · Roar Uthaug

Norway's first disaster film — a rock-slide-triggered tsunami on the Geirangerfjord. Filmed in Geiranger further north; but the geologist protagonist studied and worked in Bergen, and the Bergen University geoscience department appears in the film.
