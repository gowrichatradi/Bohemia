Day 11 · Thu 8 Oct · Bergen → Aurlandsfjord

Nærøyfjord, Undredal, Stegastein

A UNESCO fjord, a village of a hundred people, and one of the most photographed viewpoints in Europe — all inside a two-hour radius.

Tvindefossen

152 m · roadside since the 19th century

Tvindefossen has appeared on postcards since the beginning of Norwegian tourism in the late 1800s. It is a multi-strand cascade dropping 152 m in stages, right beside the E16, and has been famous partly because for a long time it was one of the few large waterfalls in Norway easy for coach tours to reach.

Speciality a local legend claims the water is medicinal — it has been used in bottling operations since the 1930s, and hydration tour buses still stop for a fill-up.

Stalheimskleiva

13 hairpins · opened 1849 · one-way downhill only

The Stalheim Road was blasted out of a cliff wall between 1842 and 1849 as the main postal and stagecoach route between Christiania (Oslo) and Bergen. Its 13 hairpin turns drop 250 m in less than 1.5 kilometres. It was superseded by a modern tunnel in 1980, and now runs as a scenic one-way road downhill through summer only, closing with the first snow.

Notice Stalheim Hotel at the top has been in continuous operation since 1885 and is where the postal riders changed horses. Kaiser Wilhelm II holidayed here every summer from 1890 to 1914.

Nærøyfjord

UNESCO 2005 · 17 km long · 250 m at narrowest · walls to 1,400 m

Nærøyfjord — a branch of the Sognefjord — was inscribed with Geirangerfjord as UNESCO West Norwegian Fjords in 2005. It is the narrowest fjord in the country: at points only 250 metres across, with sheer walls rising well over a kilometre. Farming clung to tiny grass shelves along the shore into the 20th century, some reachable only by ladder from the sea below.

Notice the abandoned farms at Styvi and Bleiklindi — accessible only by boat — are what UNESCO cites in its listing as evidence of the "cultural landscape" of the fjord. The Nærøyfjord ferry from Gudvangen still runs.

Undredal

Population ~100 · smallest still-in-use stave church in Scandinavia

Undredal is a tiny village on the Aurlandsfjord where the stave church, built in 1147, is claimed to be the smallest still in regular use in Scandinavia. The village has been famous for its brown goat cheese — geitost — for centuries; production continues today at Undredal Stølsysteri, one of the last hand-produced goat-cheese operations in the country.

Speciality Undredal geitost has EU-registered Protected Geographical Indication. There are more goats than people in the village.

Otternes Bygdatun

27 restored farm buildings · farmed 1600s–1990s

A cluster farm high on a terrace above the Aurlandsfjord, Otternes was lived in continuously from the early 17th century until the last farmer left in 1998. The buildings — barns, byres, storehouses, dwelling houses — are grey unpainted timber with turf roofs, in the traditional Sogn cluster arrangement. The site is now a preserved museum run by volunteers.

Notice the terrace was a working farm within your lifetime — the byres still smell of hay. The path from the road is a five-minute walk.

Flåm & Ægir Bryggeri

Flåm station 1940 · brewery 2007

Flåm was a small farming settlement at the head of the Aurlandsfjord until the Flåm Railway made it a tourist port. Ægir Bryggeri opened in 2007 in a purpose-built stave-church-shaped brewhouse; the beers are Norse-themed and it holds a rare Norwegian craft-brewing licence with a taproom on site.

Notice the interior copies the internal ring of a stave church — carved posts, an open fire in the middle, timber gables — a modern building using medieval stave logic.

Stegastein

Opened 2006 · 640 m above the Aurlandsfjord

Stegastein is a 30-metre timber-and-steel viewing platform cantilevered out from the Aurlandsfjellet mountainside, part of the Norwegian Scenic Routes programme launched in the 1990s. It was designed by Todd Saunders (Canadian, Bergen-based) and Tommie Wilhelmsen. The glass end wall creates the illusion of stepping into open air.

Speciality the platform faces west down the fjord, so it catches golden-hour sun on the fjord walls — the reason it appears on so many "world's best viewpoint" lists.
