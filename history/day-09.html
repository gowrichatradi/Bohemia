Day 9 · Tue 6 Oct · Oslo → Bergen

The Bergensbanen

A single seven-hour ride across the roof of Norway. Every station on the way sits on a story about how the railway got there in 1909.

The Bergensbanen (Bergen Line)

Opened 27 November 1909 · 496 km · King Haakon VII inaugurated

The Bergen Line was Norway's largest engineering project of the 19th century — 25 years of tunnelling and 182 tunnels in total — and connected Oslo to Bergen across the Hardangervidda, the largest high mountain plateau in Europe. When it opened in 1909 it cut the journey from days to hours and integrated the west coast into the young nation. The highest point is 1,237 m at Finse.

Speciality the line is single-track for nearly its whole length, with passing loops at stations. That's why the timetable holds — miss the westbound slot and there isn't another Bergensbanen for hours.

Hallingdal (Flå, Gol, Ål)

Stave church country · 12th–13th c.

The valley the train follows out of Oslo is Hallingdal — the heartland of Norwegian stave church survival. Torpo (near Ål) has one from around 1192; Gol's original was moved to the folk museum in Oslo in the 1880s and replaced with a reconstruction that still stands. The dark-timber farmhouses along the tracks are 18th- and 19th-century Hallingdal vernacular.

Notice the traditional dance form Hallingen came from this valley — an acrobatic solo dance from the 18th century in which a young man kicks a hat from a stick held high overhead.

Geilo

Ski resort since 1909 · station opened with the line

Geilo was a farming hamlet until the railway made it reachable. The first ski hotel opened alongside the station in 1909, and Geilo became Norway's first ski resort — a decade before Chamonix hosted its first winter olympics. The town has grown up around the railway station, and the platform is still its centre of gravity.

Notice the Ustedalsfjorden lies just south of the tracks — a long thin lake that Geilo's tourism was built to look at.

Finse

1,222 m · highest railway station in northern Europe · no road access

Finse sits above the treeline on the Hardangervidda and is not connected to any road — the only way in is by train, foot or ski. It became a training ground for polar explorers: Scott, Shackleton and Amundsen all tested equipment on the plateau. In 1979, the exteriors for The Empire Strikes Back's ice planet Hoth were shot at the Finse glacier.

Speciality Finse 1222 — the hotel on the platform, opened in 1909 with the line — is still running. It heats with hydro from the plateau's meltwater.

Myrdal & the Flåm Line branch

Flåm Line opened 1940 · 20 km · 866 m drop

Myrdal is where the Flåm Line branches south down to Flåm on the Aurlandsfjord — 20 kilometres of track dropping 866 metres, one of the steepest standard-gauge railways in the world. Construction took twenty years, delayed by the depression and the war; it opened in 1940. The Flåm Line was electrified from day one, powered by the very hydro-power the Bergensbanen made possible.

Notice from Myrdal westbound, the Bergensbanen enters the long Gravehalsen tunnel (5.3 km, opened 1908) — dark for four minutes, then Voss.

Voss

Old bishopric · station opened 1883 (Vossebanen)

The Vossebanen from Bergen to Voss opened in 1883, long before the full Bergen Line was completed — this stretch of the line is 26 years older than the rest of it. Voss was a medieval trading post and church centre; the stone church of St. Olav here dates to about 1277 and survived a direct bombing in 1940.

Speciality Vossakorv — the smoked mutton sausage from Voss — has EU-registered Protected Designation of Origin, one of Norway's few PDO-registered foods.
