Day 12 · Fri 9 Oct · Aurlandsfjord → Hardanger → Bergen airport

The Snow Road, apples, and a walk behind a waterfall

You cross Hardanger — Norway's orchard country — on the way south. Every cider producer on today's map is working land that was cleared for fruit in the Middle Ages.

Did you knowHardangerfjord apples ripen in September because the fjord water acts as a giant heat store — nights are milder here than 100 km inland. Cistercian monks from Lyse Abbey introduced grafted French and English apple stock in the 1200s; some Hardanger apple varieties (Aroma, Gravenstein) can still be traced back to those original imports.

Aurlandsfjellet Snow Road

Fv243 · National Scenic Route · summer only

The Aurlandsfjellet crosses the plateau at 1,300 m between Aurland and Lærdal. The road was built as a summer alternative to the longer coastal route, and reopened in 2006 with a series of Norwegian Scenic Routes interventions — the Vedahaugane rest area's minimalist concrete platforms are the most-photographed. The road closes with the first heavy snow, usually in October.

Notice the stone cairns along the route — some marked, some ancient — were built by farmers and hunters as landmarks for a summer road that predates the modern one by centuries.

Hardangerfjord

179 km long · Norway's second-longest fjord · settled since 5000 BCE

Hardanger is Norway's fruit basket. The southeast-facing slopes and mild fjord air have supported cider apple growing since Cistercian monks brought grafted varieties from Britain and France in the 13th century — the Lysekloster monastery at Osterøy is credited with the introduction. Hardanger is now the northernmost commercial fruit-growing region in the world.

Speciality Hardanger cider (sider) received EU Protected Designation of Origin status in 2019 — the same protection that champagne enjoys. Only apples from Hardanger, pressed and fermented there, can carry the name.

Ulvik

Fruit village · population ~1,000 · Ulvik Frukt & Cideri

Ulvik sits at the head of its own fjord arm and is ringed by orchards. Cider making here goes back generations, though commercial production only took off in the 2000s when Norway relaxed licensing rules on farm alcohol. Ulvik was the birthplace of the poet Olav H. Hauge (1908–1994), whose small house — now a museum — is preserved on the harbour.

Speciality Syse Gård is a working orchard, cidery and farm café run by the Syse family since the 1720s. The tasting room pours their cider poured against a view of the trees it came from.

Steinsdalsfossen

50 m fall · walkway behind cut in 1893

Steinsdalsfossen has drawn visitors since Kaiser Wilhelm II first walked behind it during his 1893 summer visit — a made path was cut into the rock face specifically so he could do it dry. The path is now the standard way to experience the fall, and it is one of the very few "walk behind" waterfalls anywhere that is genuinely dry, pushchair-friendly and free.

Notice the fall's shape is caused by a layer of hard schist above softer stone — the water dropped through faster, cutting the alcove that the path uses.

Norheimsund & the Hardanger Bridge

Bridge opened 2013 · 1,380 m main span

The Hardanger Bridge — the longest suspension bridge in Norway when it opened in 2013 — replaced the ferry between Bruravik and Brimnes and cut hours off the Bergen–Oslo drive. Norheimsund, on the western side, is home to the Hardanger Maritime Centre, which still builds traditional wooden Hardanger boats using techniques unchanged for 200 years.

Notice the bridge is designed to visually recess into the fjord — the towers are unusually slim for the span, and were painted a dull grey rather than a highlighting colour.

Unique · The old Måbødalen road

A place most people miss

The pre-1980s Rv7 from the plateau down to the fjord — 125 hairpin turns and stone tunnels blasted through mountainside. Replaced by the modern route and now open only to walkers and cyclists. Two-hour walk from Fossli Hotel.

Photo tip · Steinsdalsfossen from behind

The shot to actually take

Shoot outward through the falling water. A 1–2 second exposure blurs the water while the stones stay sharp — brace against the tunnel wall or bring a small tripod.

For the little one

The three-year-old's version of today

Steinsdalsfossen is the one. You walk behind an actual waterfall on a made railed path — no scramble, no danger, and it doubles as an umbrella for the last few metres. Twenty minutes and she'll remember it for years.

On screen · Hardanger & the plateau

Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows

IMDb 7.7 · 2010 · David Yates

Second-unit landscape shots — including the mountain-tent sequences — were filmed in Norway's Hardangervidda. The plateau itself is a national park and the largest above-treeline area in Northern Europe.

Ragnarok

IMDb 7.4 · 2020–2023 · Netflix

The fictional town of Edda is Odda — a real town at the southern end of the Hardangerfjord, about 90 minutes from Ulvik. Every school scene, every mountain climb, every fjord shot is Odda.

Trollhunter (Trolljegeren)

IMDb 7.0 · 2010 · André Øvredal

Norwegian found-footage cult classic. Filmed largely across the Norwegian mountain plateaus you're driving through today. The Ringebu troll sequence uses the same alpine scenery as the Aurlandsfjellet Snow Road.
