Day 18 · Thu 15 Oct · Prague → Dresden

The Elbe skyline

You cross into Saxony and arrive at the Baroque skyline the Allies bombed to ruins in February 1945. Every stone on tonight's shoot is either 18th-century or reconstructed since 1990.

Did you knowThe Fürstenzug's 25,000 porcelain tiles survived the 1945 firestorm because porcelain doesn't burn. When Dresden's old town was flattened around it, the wall stood almost untouched, protecting the tiles behind it. Six tiles were damaged. It is the largest work of art that came through Dresden's destruction essentially intact.

Dresden's Altstadt

Rebuilt 1990s–2020s from 1945 rubble · "Florence of the Elbe"

On 13 and 14 February 1945 Allied bombing and the resulting firestorm destroyed roughly 90% of central Dresden. What you see now is a mix of surviving stone, salvaged sculpture and near-total reconstruction. Reunification made the money and the political will available in the 1990s, and the last major reconstruction — the Neumarkt around the Frauenkirche — was only completed in the 2020s. Dresden earned its "Florence on the Elbe" nickname in the 18th century under August the Strong; the reconstruction has restored the outline of that city.

Notice the reconstruction incorporates original stones where possible — blackened by fire and centuries of soot — set into new pale sandstone. You can read the ratio of old to new on almost every facade.

Fürstenzug (Procession of Princes)

102 m long · 25,000 Meissen porcelain tiles · applied 1904–1907

The Fürstenzug is a mural of 35 Saxon rulers processing on horseback across the wall of the Stallhof, the former royal stables. Originally painted in 1876 in sgraffito, it was replaced between 1904 and 1907 by 25,000 tiles from Meissen — the porcelain manufactory two hours down the Elbe. Its 102-metre length makes it the world's largest porcelain artwork. Remarkably it survived the 1945 firestorm almost intact — porcelain shrugs off fire — while the buildings around it burnt.

Speciality the mural depicts every Saxon ruler from 1127 to 1904 (Wettin dynasty) in period costume, with servants and artists among them, walking together in one continuous scene. Look for the artist at the end — Wilhelm Walther, who designed the piece, and inserted himself as the last figure in the procession.

Brühl's Terrace

Opened to the public 1814 · nicknamed "the Balcony of Europe"

The terrace runs 500 metres along the Elbe on top of the old city fortifications. Heinrich von Brühl — the powerful minister of August III — took over this stretch of city wall for his private garden in the 18th century. When the fortifications lost their military purpose after Napoleon, the terrace was opened to the public in 1814. Goethe called it "the Balcony of Europe".

Notice the four sculpture groups at the corners of the terrace — Morning, Noon, Evening and Night — are Johannes Schilling's, 1868, and among Dresden's most photographed public sculptures.

Augustusbrücke

Baroque bridge 1727–1731 · rebuilt 1907 & 1949

The current Augustusbrücke is the fifth bridge on this site — the earliest dates from around 1275, and each successive rebuild was widened for more traffic. The Baroque bridge of Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann (1727–1731) was the most beautiful; it was replaced in 1907 with the current stone arches, dynamited on the last day of WWII, then repaired in 1949. The classic Dresden skyline photograph — Frauenkirche, Hofkirche, Ständehaus — is shot from this bridge.

Notice the bridge is now pedestrian and tram only — it was closed to cars in 2017, giving photographers permanent standing room in the middle.

Dresden Parkeisenbahn (Park Railway)

Opened 1950 · staffed by children · 5.6 km narrow-gauge in the Großer Garten

The Dresden Park Railway has run since 1950 as a "children's railway" — a narrow-gauge steam-and-diesel railway inside the Großer Garten staffed almost entirely by children aged 10 to 16, under adult supervision. They serve as guards, ticket-sellers, signalmen and station masters. It is one of very few surviving children's railways in Europe — most were an East Bloc pedagogical tradition.

Speciality the children rotate through every job on the railway across a season, and the older ones learn to drive the trains. The engineering is real: real signals, real timetable, real locomotives.

On screen · Dresden & Saxony

Inglourious Basterds

IMDb 8.4 · 2009 · Quentin Tarantino

The cinema in the film's final act — where the Nazi officers meet their end — was built in Görlitz, an hour east of Dresden. Christoph Waltz's Colonel Landa (Oscar for Best Supporting Actor) drives past Saxon Baroque facades in several sequences. Nearby Bad Schandau and Saxon Switzerland also appear.

The Grand Budapest Hotel

IMDb 8.1 · 2014 · Wes Anderson · 9 Oscar nominations

Anderson filmed almost the entire movie in Görlitz, an hour east of Dresden — one of the few German cities where the pre-war Jugendstil facades survived WWII intact. Görlitz has hosted so many Hollywood shoots (Inglourious Basterds, The Reader) that locals call it 'Görliwood'.

The Reader

IMDb 7.6 · 2008 · Stephen Daldry · Kate Winslet · Oscar for Best Actress

Filmed extensively in Görlitz and Berlin. Winslet's Hanna Schmitz walks streets that appear in Grand Budapest as different places entirely — the same architecture standing in for two very different fictional worlds.
